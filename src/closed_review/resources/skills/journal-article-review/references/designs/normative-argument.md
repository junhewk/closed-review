# Normative or conceptual argument

## Recognize

Cues: a thesis about what ought to be done, what a concept means, or whether a position is
justified; reasoning from premises, cases, theories, or principles. When the argument also rests on
the authors' own empirical data, this is empirical bioethics: load the empirical profile as well and
use the last section of this profile.

The reviewer judges the quality of the argument, not whether they share its conclusion. Keep that
distinction visible in every candidate.

## Where it usually breaks

### Thesis and contribution
- Signals: no statable thesis, or a different thesis in the abstract and the conclusion; no clear
  advance over existing positions; the opposing view presented in a weak form while stronger versions
  exist in the literature.
- Would settle it: a one-sentence thesis and an account of what it adds to the named debate.

### Concepts
- Signals: a central term (autonomy, harm, dignity, best interests, vulnerability, family) shifts
  meaning between premises; a stipulative definition that quietly does the argument's work; a
  conceptual analysis without testing against counterexamples.

### Structure and validity
- Signals: a conclusion that does not follow without an unstated premise; a slippery slope with no
  account of why the slope is likely (empirical) or unavoidable (logical); appeals to intuition
  without saying whose intuitions or why they should count; thought experiments that differ from the
  real case in a morally relevant way.
- Would settle it: a reconstruction of the main argument with each premise stated. Offer one in the
  candidate when it helps the reviewer see the gap.

### Empirical premises
- Signals: factual claims without sources, outdated clinical or legal facts, or a normative
  conclusion that rests on an empirical premise stronger than its evidence.
- Would settle it: sources for each premise; a version of the conclusion conditional on the fact.

### Objections and replies
- Signals: the strongest objections missing; replies that restate the thesis rather than answer the
  objection; an objection answered in a weak form.

### Theory, framework, and context
- Applies to any approach: consequentialist (including utilitarian), deontological (including
  Kantian), contractualist and other theories of justice (for example Rawlsian or capability
  approaches), virtue ethics, care ethics, casuistry, principlism, Confucian or other relational
  approaches.
- Signals: a theory invoked but not applied; the conclusion depends on the choice of theory but the
  choice is never defended; standard objections to the theory that bear on this application ignored
  (for example aggregation in a utilitarian argument about allocation, or conflicting duties in a
  deontological one); principles used as a checklist without specifying or balancing them; cultural
  or jurisdictional assumptions (for example family-centered decision-making) treated as premises
  without defense.

### From conclusion to recommendation
- Signals: policy or practice recommendations that need premises about feasibility, institutions, or
  consequences that the paper never defends; a conclusion stronger than the argument supports.
- Proportionate fixes: state the bridging premises, or narrow the recommendation.

### Use of the literature
- Signals: cited authors represented in ways their work may not support; central contributions to the
  debate missing. Treat any recollection of what a source says as `model_lead` and ask the reviewer.

## When the argument uses the authors' own data

Load the empirical profile for the data (`qualitative`, or the general rubric's quantitative section
for surveys and other quantitative designs). The empirical profile judges the data; the sections
above judge the argument; this section judges how the two are joined. A paper that only describes
what people think and draws no normative conclusion is descriptive ethics: use the empirical profile
alone and check only that its conclusions stay descriptive.

### Aim
- Signals: aims stated descriptively while conclusions are normative, or the reverse; unclear whether
  the data are meant to inform, test, or ground the normative claim.

### Integration
- Signals: no account of how findings and normative reasoning were brought together; a normative
  discussion attached after the results with no link to them; an integration method (reflective
  equilibrium, dialogical or deliberative approaches, grounded moral analysis, and others) named but
  not visible in the analysis; unclear who integrates (researchers alone, or participants).
- Would settle it: where integration happens (design, analysis, or conclusions) and how, with one
  example of a finding changing or supporting a normative claim.

### Role of the data
- Signals: no explanation of why these data bear on the normative question (are views treated as
  moral intuitions, as evidence of interests, as constraints on feasibility?); a role that shifts
  between sections.

### From data to norms
- Signals: a majority view treated as the right answer; dissenting or minority views dropped rather
  than weighed; a normative conclusion that needs premises the paper never states; context-bound
  findings generalized into universal norms.
- Proportionate fixes: state the additional premises; weigh minority views explicitly; limit the
  normative claim to the context studied.

### Team and reflexivity
- Signals: the researchers' normative commitments shaping questions, sampling, or interpretation
  without acknowledgment; expertise on only one side of the empirical-normative divide.

## Common false alarms

- Asking for a methods section, empirical data, or a limitations section in the format of an
  empirical paper, when the paper has no data of its own.
- Rejecting the paper because the reviewer, or the model, disagrees with the conclusion.
- Faulting the choice of one theory over another, unless the conclusion depends on it undefended.
- Asking the authors to stay neutral on a contested question; taking a position is the genre.
- Asking for more literature review where the argument is already situated adequately.
- Calling reflective equilibrium circular because the considered judgments and the principles agree.
  In narrow and wide reflective equilibrium, considered judgments, principles, and background
  theories are adjusted against one another; none is a fixed input or output, and coherence among
  them is the aim (Rawls 1971; Daniels 1979). Ask instead whether the paper shows any adjustment and
  which judgments or principles it revised.
- Asking a proposal stated as principles for operational rules, review procedures, or
  implementation detail that it leaves to later policy work.
- Asking for evidence of contextual background that readers in the field take as given (for
  example how a national insurance system governs practice), when the conclusion does not turn on
  it.
- With the authors' own data: insisting on one correct integration method (there is no consensus);
  faulting descriptive ethics for not reaching normative conclusions it never claims; treating the
  is-ought gap as fatal in itself rather than asking how the paper bridges it.

## Monitoring areas

Thesis and contribution; concepts; structure and validity; empirical premises; objections and
replies; theory and context; recommendations; use of the literature. With the authors' own data,
also: aim; integration; role of the data; from data to norms; team and reflexivity.
