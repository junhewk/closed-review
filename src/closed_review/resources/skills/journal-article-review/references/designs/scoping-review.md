# Scoping review

## Recognize

Cues: a population-concept-context (PCC) question, a broad search, a charting form, descriptive
results about what kinds of evidence, concepts, or gaps exist. A review titled "scoping" that
searches unsystematically is a narrative review; one that weighs effects is a systematic review.
Either mismatch is itself a possible candidate.

## Where it usually breaks

### Why a scoping review
- Look in: introduction and aims.
- Signals: aims about whether something works, how well, or which option is better; "scoping" chosen
  apparently to avoid appraisal or synthesis that the question needs.
- Would settle it: aims stated as mapping (types of evidence, concepts, characteristics, gaps), or a
  change of review type.

### Concept and eligibility
- Look in: PCC, eligibility criteria, definitions.
- Signals: the central concept is undefined or defined differently across the paper, so the map has
  no clear boundary; eligibility changed during the review without documentation.
- Would settle it: an operational definition and a record of iterative changes.

### Protocol and transparency
- Look in: methods, registration (often OSF or a protocol publication).
- Signals: no a priori protocol; deviations unreported.

### Search and selection
- Look in: methods, strategies, flow diagram.
- Signals: breadth claims not matched by sources (missing field-specific or regional databases, grey
  literature where the concept lives in policy or practice documents); single screening without
  justification; flow numbers that do not add up.

### Charting
- Look in: methods, charting form, supplements.
- Signals: no charting form or no piloting; charted variables that do not answer the review
  questions; single charting without verification.
- Would settle it: the form as a supplement, the piloting process.

### Analysis and presentation
- Look in: results, tables, figures.
- Signals: results that only list included studies; claims of gaps that may reflect the search rather
  than the literature; statements about what "the evidence shows" for effects; qualitative content
  analysis claimed without describing it.
- Would settle it: a numerical summary plus a described qualitative analysis aligned with the
  questions; gap claims qualified by the search's limits.

### Implications
- Look in: discussion and conclusions.
- Signals: recommendations for practice or policy presented as if the review graded evidence.
  Implications for research and for how a field defines its terms are appropriate.

## Major flaws

Major when the quoted evidence shows them (see the index).

- **wrong-review-type**: aims about whether something works, how well, or which option is better,
  answered with a scoping review.
- **unbounded-concept**: the central concept is undefined or defined differently across the paper,
  so the map has no clear boundary.
- **effect-claims**: statements about what the evidence shows for effects, or practice or policy
  recommendations presented as if the review graded evidence.
- **single-review**: single screening or single charting without justification, or described
  elsewhere in the paper as independent duplicate review.
- **double-counting**: two or more included sources are reports of the same study, trial, or
  sample (the same registration, cohort, or participants), counted as separate sources in a count,
  proportion, or claim about how much evidence exists.

## Common false alarms

- Asking for critical appraisal of included sources. It is optional; if it was done, ask only that it
  be used coherently.
- Asking for a meta-analysis or a GRADE assessment.
- Asking for PROSPERO registration, which does not accept scoping reviews.
- Treating the optional consultation stage as required.
- Criticizing breadth or heterogeneity of included sources, which is often the point.

## Monitoring areas

Rationale for review type; concept and eligibility; protocol; search and selection; charting;
analysis and presentation; implications.
