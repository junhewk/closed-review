# General peer-review rubric

Apply only checks that fit the paper. Mark non-applicable items explicitly rather than manufacturing
criticism.

## Shared questions

- Is the research question or central claim clear, worthwhile, and matched by the work performed?
- Does the framing represent relevant alternatives and distinguish evidence from assertion?
- Are design, sampling, materials, measures, procedures, and analytic choices fit for the claims?
- Can a reader understand and, where appropriate, reproduce the work from the supplied detail?
- Do reported numbers, tables, figures, text, and supplements agree?
- Do conclusions follow from the results, acknowledge uncertainty, and avoid causal or general claims
  beyond the design?
- Are limitations, ethics, consent, conflicts, funding, data/code availability, and AI use addressed
  when applicable?
- Are references used accurately and are journal-specific requirements satisfied?

## Article-type emphasis

When the reviewer confirms a design profile from [designs/index.md](designs/index.md), use it in
place of the matching paragraph below. These paragraphs remain the fallback for other designs.

### Quantitative empirical work

Check estimands, comparison groups, eligibility, missing data, multiplicity, model assumptions,
effect sizes with uncertainty, sensitivity analyses, and consistency between reported sample sizes.
Recalculate only when all needed values are present.

### Qualitative empirical work

Check sampling logic, researcher position, data generation, analytic process, reflexivity, saturation
or information power claims, evidentiary excerpts, negative cases, and the connection between themes
and conclusions.

### Mixed-methods work

Assess each component and whether integration is designed, executed, and interpreted coherently.

### Systematic or scoping review

Check question framing, protocol/registration, search reproducibility, eligibility, duplicate
screening, study linkage, extraction, appraisal, synthesis choice, heterogeneity, missing outcomes,
certainty, and whether conclusions match the included evidence.

### Theoretical, humanities, or commentary work

Check the thesis, conceptual definitions, argumentative validity, treatment of counterarguments,
selection and interpretation of sources, historical/contextual accuracy, and whether normative and
empirical claims are distinguished.

## Candidate quality

Use this rubric to discover possibilities, never to manufacture or pre-accept a fixed list. A useful
candidate states the potential problem, why it changes interpretation or usability, the exact
location, relevant literature context with its verification tier, and proportionate solution
options. It becomes a review comment only after the reviewer develops and accepts it.

Major candidates could affect the contribution, validity, evidence, interpretation, ethics, or
ability to evaluate the work. Minor candidates are local corrections that do not determine the
central judgment. Call a candidate major only when you can name the specific conclusion of the paper
that would not follow; expect few.

### Checks before keeping a candidate

Argue the authors' side first. Write the strongest reply the authors could give from their own
manuscript; if it answers the concern, drop the concern. In particular:

- **Search before claiming absence.** "Not explained", "not justified", or "no evidence" holds only
  if the answer is nowhere in the manuscript: other sections, tables, notes, and the stated criteria
  count. A rationale that follows from criteria the paper states is a rationale.
- **A stated scope or limitation answers the concern.** When the paper says what it compares,
  excludes, or leaves to later work, do not ask for the excluded thing. Raise it only if you can say
  concretely why the statement is insufficient for a conclusion the paper draws, and then as minor.
- **Stay within the paper's scope.** Do not ask a principles-level or conceptual proposal for
  implementation detail, operational rules, or data that belong to later policy work or another
  study, unless a stated conclusion depends on them.
- **Shared background needs no proof.** Contextual facts about the setting that readers in the
  field take as given do not need evidence, unless the conclusion turns on a contested empirical
  claim.
- **Only the authors' defects are candidates.** That you cannot check a cited source is a
  verification task for the reviewer, not a request to the authors. Do not ask for more precise
  quotation of sources the paper already cites with locations.
- **Know the method before faulting it.** Describe the method as its own literature does before
  criticizing its use; a criticism that rests on a misdescription of the method is not a candidate.
- **Do not supply the context you lack.** If the concern depends on how a legal system, health
  system, or professional practice works and neither the paper nor the reviewer has established
  that, do not build a candidate on an alternative reading. Ask the reviewer one short question, or
  drop it.
- **A concern that concedes its own weak point is usually not one.** If writing the candidate
  requires "although the authors state…", check whether that statement already answers it.
