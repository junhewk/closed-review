# Design profiles

A design profile says, for one kind of manuscript, where that kind of work usually fails, which
flaws are major in it, how to tell a real problem from a false alarm, and the areas to monitor. It
is an inspection aid, not a list of review comments: nothing in a profile becomes a candidate until
the manuscript gives evidence for it. Apply only what fits the paper; say when an item does not
apply rather than manufacturing criticism.

## Choosing profiles

After reading the manuscript and before presenting the first candidate, propose the profiles that fit
and give a one-line reason for each, citing what in the manuscript shows the design. Ask the reviewer
to confirm or correct the choice, in chat or with clarify, then read only the chosen profiles; if no
one can answer, use your proposal and say so. Load at most three. If none fits, say so and use the
shared questions alone.

A design the authors label one way but carry out another is itself a possible candidate. Propose the
profile for the design actually performed and note the mismatch.

| Profile | Use for |
|---|---|
| [qualitative](qualitative.md) | Interview, focus group, observation, or document studies analyzed qualitatively |
| [quantitative](quantitative.md) | Surveys, vignette studies, observational studies of records or practice, trials of ethics interventions, and prediction-model studies |
| [systematic-review](systematic-review.md) | Systematic reviews and meta-analyses, qualitative evidence syntheses, reviews of reasons or normative literature |
| [scoping-review](scoping-review.md) | Scoping reviews and evidence or concept maps |
| [normative-argument](normative-argument.md) | Philosophical, bioethical, or conceptual arguments, including the normative part of empirical bioethics |
| [legal-analysis](legal-analysis.md) | Doctrinal, comparative, or law-reform analysis of statutes, case law, or regulation |

Profiles combine. Load each profile whose kind of work the manuscript contains:

- Empirical bioethics with interview or other qualitative data: `qualitative` and
  `normative-argument`. Its section on the authors' own data covers how the two are joined.
- Empirical bioethics with a survey or other quantitative data: `quantitative` and
  `normative-argument`.
- Descriptive ethics (what people think, with no normative conclusion): the empirical profile
  alone.
- Mixed methods: `qualitative` and `quantitative`, and ask whether the integration is designed,
  carried out, and interpreted coherently.
- Law-reform proposal: `legal-analysis` and `normative-argument`.
- Review of reasons that ends in recommendations: `systematic-review` and `normative-argument`.

## Using a profile

- "Major flaws" lists what is major in that design, beside the "Major flaws in any design" below.
  An entry is an instance only when its quoted evidence shows the flaw: the list judges severity;
  it is not a source of candidates.
- Check "Common false alarms" before surfacing a candidate. If the concern matches one, drop it or
  reframe it as the profile says.
- Guidelines and methods sources a profile names are pointers for the reviewer. Label them
  `model_lead` unless the reviewer supplies the source, and never attribute content to them from
  memory as if it were checked.
- Journal instructions and the reviewer's view override a profile.

## Shared questions

With or without a profile: Is the question or central claim clear, worthwhile, and matched by the
work? Are the design, sample, materials, and analysis fit for the claims, and described well enough
to follow? Do the numbers, tables, text, and supplements agree? Do the conclusions follow, with
their uncertainty, and without causal or general claims beyond the design? Are limitations, ethics,
consent, conflicts, funding, and data availability addressed? Are references used accurately?

## Major flaws in any design

These are major in any design, with or without a profile, when the manuscript shows them. Flaws
that depend on the design (causal claims from associations, claims beyond what a sample, data, or
argument supports) are in each profile's own list.

- **self-contradiction**: the paper states two incompatible things about how the study was done or
  what it found (for example a procedure in the methods and a different safeguard in the
  limitations), and a result or conclusion depends on which is true. Two values of a stated detail
  (a date, a count, a label, a citation) that no conclusion rests on are not this flaw; they are a
  correction.
- **misread-source**: a source the argument relies on (a provision, decision, study, or guideline)
  is reported as saying what its own quoted text does not say.
- **research-ethics**: participants' data, samples, or records were collected or used without the
  approval, consent, or waiver the study needs, or before approval was given.
- **undisclosed-interest**: the paper shows a competing interest (funding, employment, an advisory
  role, or authorship of evaluated or included work) that its competing-interests statement does not
  declare, and the conclusions bear on that interest.

## Before keeping a candidate

A candidate is major only when you can name the conclusion of the paper that would not follow;
expect few. Minor candidates are local corrections. Argue the authors' side first: write the
strongest reply they could give from their own manuscript, and drop the concern if it answers it.

- **Search before claiming absence.** "Not explained" holds only if the answer is nowhere in the
  manuscript, including other sections, tables, notes, and the stated criteria.
- **A stated scope or limitation answers the concern.** Do not ask for what the paper says it
  excludes, unless a conclusion it draws depends on it, and then as minor.
- **Stay within the paper's scope.** Do not ask a conceptual or principles-level paper for
  implementation detail that belongs to later work.
- **Shared background needs no proof**, unless the conclusion turns on a contested empirical claim.
- **Only the authors' defects are candidates.** That you cannot check a cited source is a task for
  the reviewer, not a request to the authors.
- **Know the method before faulting it**, as its own literature describes it.
- **Do not supply the context you lack.** If the concern depends on how a legal or health system
  works and neither the paper nor the reviewer has said, ask the reviewer one short question or
  drop it.
- **A concern that needs "although the authors state..." is usually answered** by that statement.
