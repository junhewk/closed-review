# Legal analysis

## Recognize

Cues: a legal question, named jurisdictions, analysis of legal sources, a conclusion about what the
law is, how it should be read, or how it should change. Many papers mix these. Identify which
claims are about the law as it is and which about the law as it should be.

## Where it usually breaks

### Question, jurisdiction, and method
- Look in: introduction, scope statements.
- Signals: no clear legal question; jurisdiction or level (national, regional, international)
  unclear; the method (doctrinal, comparative, socio-legal, law reform) never stated, so the reader
  cannot tell what kind of claim is being made.

### Sources and currency
- Look in: citations, footnotes, discussion of provisions and decisions.
- Signals: no date stating when the law was current; reliance on secondary accounts where primary
  sources (statutes, enforcement decrees and rules, decisions) are available; provisions cited
  without article or paragraph; possible recent amendments or decisions not discussed.
- Currency cannot be verified from the manuscript or from model memory. Raise a possible change in
  the law as a question for the reviewer, labeled `model_lead`, not as a finding.

### Interpretation
- Look in: analysis of provisions and decisions.
- Signals: one reading presented as the only one; holding and obiter not distinguished, or the weight
  of decisions misstated for that legal system (for example how far supreme or constitutional court
  decisions bind in a civil-law jurisdiction); administrative interpretations or agency guidance
  treated as binding law; soft-law instruments (declarations, professional guidelines, IRB guidance)
  treated as legal obligations.
- Would settle it: the interpretive method used and the competing readings considered.

### The law as it is and as it should be
- Signals: descriptive and normative claims mixed so that criticism of the law reads as a statement
  of what it requires, or the reverse; reform proposals without the normative argument for them
  (load `normative-argument` when the proposal is central).

### Comparative analysis
- Look in: sections on foreign law.
- Signals: comparator jurisdictions chosen without reason; foreign law described from secondary or
  outdated sources; legal terms translated as if equivalent when the concepts differ (for example
  terms for life-sustaining treatment, advance directives, or self-determination); a proposal to
  transplant a rule without addressing institutional differences.
- Would settle it: a reason for the comparators, primary sources, and notes on non-equivalent terms.

### Empirical claims about law
- Signals: claims that a law caused, prevented, or changed practice without evidence; data on legal
  practice presented without method (then the matching empirical profile applies).

### Law and ethics
- Signals: legality treated as ethical justification, or ethical criticism treated as a legal
  conclusion; in an ethics or medical journal, legal reasoning a non-lawyer cannot follow.

## Major flaws

Major when the quoted evidence shows them (see the index).

- **misread-provision**: a provision or decision is reported as saying what its own quoted text does
  not say, or one reading is presented as the only one where the paper itself reports another.
- **authority-misweighted**: holding and obiter not distinguished, or the weight of decisions
  misstated for that legal system, such as a decision treated as settling a point a higher court has
  decided otherwise.
- **soft-law-as-law**: administrative guidance or soft-law instruments treated as binding legal
  obligations.
- **comparator-misdescribed**: foreign law misdescribed, or described from secondary or outdated
  sources, where the comparison depends on it.
- **reform-without-argument**: a reform proposal without the normative argument for it, or
  inconsistent with the paper's own analysis.

## Common false alarms

- Asking for an empirical methods section in a doctrinal paper.
- Treating citation-style differences (OSCOLA, Bluebook, or the journal's own) as substantive.
- Asking for exhaustive comparative coverage beyond the paper's stated scope.
- Faulting the paper for arguing for reform; normative legal scholarship is legitimate.
- Treating a reading the reviewer disputes as an error, when it is a defensible interpretation the
  paper should acknowledge as contested.
- Offering an alternative reading of what a rule's silence means (for example, that an unlisted
  item may still be covered) when how that jurisdiction reads silence is not established by the
  paper or the reviewer. The paper's reading may reflect local practice the model does not know; ask
  the reviewer instead.
- Faulting a comparison of professional guidelines with binding rules for comparing documents of
  different legal force, when the paper states that it compares clinical scope rather than
  reimbursement or legal effect.

## Monitoring areas

Question and method; sources and currency; interpretation; law as it is and should be; comparative
analysis; empirical claims; law and ethics.
