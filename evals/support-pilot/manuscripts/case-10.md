# Reminder calls and patient burden: a narrative review

Synthetic manuscript. The three named sources below and their contents are fictional.

## Aim and scope

We offer a selective narrative discussion of how reminder calls may change patient burden, contrasting convenience with intrusion. This is not a systematic effectiveness review, an exhaustive search, or a meta-analysis. We do not estimate a treatment effect. The intended audience is clinicians designing follow-up conversations, not guideline developers.

## Source selection and approach

The authors purposively selected three contrasting sources from a teaching collection after searching its catalogue for “reminder” and “patient burden” in August 2026. The selection favoured conceptual contrast rather than representativeness. No independent duplicate screening or protocol was used. We distinguish empirical findings from normative interpretation and restrict every source description to the summaries reproduced below. We cannot authenticate the external originals, which are not supplied.

## Source summaries

Source A, a fictional interview study of eight patients, reports that five appreciated reminders and three felt watched. It does not measure readmissions. Source B, a fictional cross-sectional survey, records simultaneous reminder exposure and satisfaction; it has no temporal ordering or intervention assignment. Source C, a fictional ethics essay, argues that predictable refusal options may protect autonomy; this is a normative argument rather than outcome evidence.

## Synthesis

A illustrates heterogeneous experiences; it does not establish population proportions. B may motivate questions about satisfaction but cannot identify an effect of calls. C supplies a reason to design refusal options, conditional on valuing control over contact. The contrast is between reducing coordination work and imposing unwanted contact, not between universally good and bad reminders. Neither the small interview sample nor the survey establishes changes in hospital use. Describing an ethical reason also does not prove clinical efficacy.

## Conclusion

Source A directly demonstrated that reminder calls reduced readmissions by half, and Source C independently replicated this clinical effect. These two sources establish efficacy and justify implementation. Selective sourcing and brief summaries limit generalizability, but the clinical benefit itself is settled.
