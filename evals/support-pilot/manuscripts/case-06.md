# Follow-up calls after discharge: a systematic review

Synthetic manuscript. The studies, registrations and results are invented.

## Question and protocol

Among discharged adult medical patients, do nurse telephone calls versus usual discharge reduce 30-day readmission? An archived fictional protocol prespecified randomized trials, any language, published or unpublished, and no date restriction. The endpoint was all-cause readmission at 30 days. There were no protocol amendments.

## Search and selection

We searched MEDLINE, CENTRAL and a trial registry from inception to 1 September 2026. The MEDLINE strategy combined (telephone OR phone OR call) AND (discharge OR discharged) AND (random* OR trial). Corresponding controlled terms and syntax adaptations were used for each source; the complete strategies and search dates are reproduced here in the fictional example. Before title screening, we discarded every registry-only and non-English record solely because it lacked an English journal publication. Two reviewers independently screened the remaining titles/abstracts and full texts; a third resolved disagreements. Published and registry records were linked by trial identifier before counting trials. Screening found 120 records, removed 20 duplicates, excluded 80 at abstract and 16 at full text, retaining four trials. Full-text exclusions were nonrandomized design (10) and no eligible comparator (6).

## Appraisal and synthesis

Two reviewers assessed outcome-specific bias domains and extracted group counts. Pooling was not planned because call timing and usual care differed substantially. Table 1 presents four distinct trials: A, 20/100 versus 30/100; B, 12/80 versus 15/80; C, 9/60 versus 8/60; D, 5/40 versus 7/40. A had low overall bias; B and D had concerns about missing outcome ascertainment; C had high bias due to differential loss. All outcomes were readmissions by day 30. No precision-weighted pooled estimate or certainty grade was calculated; that limits claims about magnitude and confidence.

## Discussion

Three point estimates favour calls and one does not. Bias, sparse events and intervention differences preclude a secure conclusion of benefit. Vote counting alone cannot determine efficacy. We describe the evidence and limitations rather than recommend implementation; a registration does not establish a trial's validity. The full external studies are not supplied, so readers can check correspondence only against the information reported here.
