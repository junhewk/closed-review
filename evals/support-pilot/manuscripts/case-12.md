# Permission and fairness in fictional hospital bed allocation

Synthetic normative perspective. Jurisdiction Z, its statute, cases and observations are invented. This is not legal advice.

## Question and definitions

When a hospital can allocate scarce beds by arrival order, should it do so? We distinguish “legally permitted” (not prohibited by the stipulated rule) from “ethically justified” (supported by reasons under a stated ethical framework). For this exercise, clause 4 of fictional Statute Z says: “Hospitals may use arrival order but are not required to do so.” No other law or judicial authority is supplied.

## Approach and premises

The method is a conditional normative argument, not an empirical effectiveness study or an account of real law. Premise 1: equally urgent patients deserve equal consideration. Premise 2: patients may face unequal transport delays beyond their control. Premise 3: when such delays affect arrival order, arrival alone can encode an ethically irrelevant disadvantage. The second premise is a stipulated possibility, not a measured frequency. Together these premises create a reason against relying exclusively on arrival when feasible alternatives reduce that disadvantage.

## Objection and reply

An objection favours arrival order for transparency and resistance to discretionary favouritism. We accept those as relevant values. A documented lottery among equally urgent, eligible patients may preserve transparency while reducing transport disadvantage, but its feasibility and actual effects require investigation. We do not assume it always dominates arrival order. Clinical urgency is held equal here; the argument does not extend unchanged to patients with different urgency.

## Conclusion and limits

Because Statute Z permits arrival order, it legally requires every hospital to use arrival order. That permission also proves arrival order ethically obligatory, without any additional ethical premise. Alternatives are therefore both illegal and unethical regardless of transport disadvantage. The argument depends on its fairness premise and feasibility assumptions. External legal and empirical references have not been verified.
