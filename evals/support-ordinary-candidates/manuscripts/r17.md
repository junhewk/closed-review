# Offering a structured discharge telephone call after an internal-medicine admission: a single-centre randomised study

Synthetic manuscript for software development. All people, institutions, dates, approvals and observations below are invented. This is not a report of actual research or evidence for patient care.

## Abstract

### Background

A discharge telephone programme might improve continuity by giving patients an opportunity to clarify instructions after returning home. Whether offering such a programme changes subsequent hospital use is distinct from whether patients who answer a call have better outcomes. We evaluated the offer of a structured call, rather than the effect of actually completing one.

### Methods

We individually randomised 320 adults discharged from the internal-medicine service of the fictional Alder Hospital to an offer of a structured telephone call or usual care, with 160 assigned to each group. Assignment occurred immediately before discharge. The primary outcome was the proportion of participants with at least one unplanned hospital readmission within 30 days, obtained for all randomised participants from the linked registry assumed in this synthetic setting. We compared unweighted participant counts using a two-sided Pearson chi-square test without continuity correction and reported the unadjusted risk ratio with a log-scale 95% confidence interval. A secondary, exploratory readiness questionnaire was available for 152 intervention and 154 usual-care participants; its means were compared using an independent-samples Welch t test.

### Results

A live intervention conversation occurred for 154 of the 160 participants offered a call; 148 completed the script and 6 had an interrupted conversation. Primary outcome ascertainment was complete for all 320 participants. At least one unplanned readmission occurred in 24/160 intervention participants (15.0%) and 40/160 usual-care participants (25.0%). The unadjusted risk ratio was 0.60 (95% CI 0.38 to 0.95); Pearson chi-square was 5.000 with 1 degree of freedom, p=0.025. Mean readiness scores were 7.4 (SD 2.1, n=152) and 6.8 (SD 2.2, n=154); the exploratory mean difference was 0.60 (95% CI 0.12 to 1.08), with Welch t=2.441, 303.662 degrees of freedom, p=0.015.

### Conclusions

In this single-centre synthetic study, offering the programme was associated with fewer participants experiencing a readmission over 30 days. Random allocation supports an intention-to-treat comparison within the study setting, but the small feasibility-driven sample and setting-specific outcome infrastructure limit broader conclusions. The questionnaire result is exploratory, and its clinical importance has not been established. These findings would justify further evaluation, not a general recommendation to introduce the programme.

## Introduction

The transition from hospital to home involves several different tasks. Patients may need to understand which instructions apply immediately, distinguish routine follow-up from an urgent problem, arrange transport, and identify whom to contact when written information is unclear. A telephone programme could plausibly help with some of these tasks without addressing all causes of a subsequent admission. Conversely, a conversation that improves understanding might have little effect on hospital use when readmission is driven by disease progression or by circumstances outside the programme's scope. We therefore treated a potential effect on readmission as a question to investigate, rather than as an established property of telephone contact.

The intervention was designed around clarification and access to existing services. It did not introduce a new treatment, replace a clinical assessment, or authorise the caller to change medicines. The proposed mechanism was that a patient who could explain the discharge plan and locate an appropriate contact would be better able to use the services already available. This mechanism is a design hypothesis. The study did not independently measure each step in the mechanism, and a difference in the primary outcome would not by itself demonstrate that improved understanding caused it.

An important design distinction concerns the population being compared. People who can be reached by telephone may differ from those who cannot, including in their availability, support and preferences. Restricting analysis to people who completed a conversation would therefore change the question and could undermine the benefit of random assignment. Our primary estimand was the effect of being offered the programme in the discharge process, regardless of subsequent contact. All outcome follow-up began at discharge, including for events that happened before a scheduled call.

This was a preliminary service evaluation with a fixed recruitment capacity. Its purpose was to estimate an outcome contrast under a clearly described delivery arrangement and to document whether the offer could be implemented. It was not designed to establish effectiveness across hospitals or to identify an optimal call script. The primary objective was the between-group contrast in the risk of at least one unplanned readmission during the first 30 days. Secondary objectives were descriptive assessment of call delivery and an exploratory comparison of reported readiness. We did not specify subgroup effectiveness claims or a mediation analysis.

## Methods

### Setting, eligibility and recruitment

The fictional Alder Hospital is a single general hospital with one participating adult internal-medicine discharge service. Recruitment took place from 1 February through 30 September 2025. Adults aged at least 18 years were eligible when discharged to a community address within the linked-registry region and able to receive a call themselves or through a nominated care partner. An eligible person could decline contact without affecting usual care. Discharges to another inpatient institution and admissions outside the service's recruitment window were not eligible index events. Each person could enter the study once; a later hospital admission did not create another study participant.

Research staff screened the discharge list before approaching patients. Written consent covered random assignment, the optional contact and linkage to the outcome registry. Where a person could not provide consent and no authorised representative was available within the discharge window, that person was not enrolled. The implications of this criterion for representation are considered below. Eligibility did not depend on owning a particular device or completing the secondary questionnaire. The study team recorded reasons for exclusion in mutually exclusive categories, using the first applicable reason in the screening record.

### Allocation and masking

A statistician not involved in recruitment generated a computerised 1:1 allocation sequence using randomly varied block sizes. Recruiters entered consent and eligibility before an electronic system revealed assignment. The sequence and future assignments were not available to staff recruiting participants. Randomisation occurred immediately before discharge, after the routine discharge documentation had been completed. The participant, discharge team and caller could not be masked to an offer of a telephone conversation. Registry outcome extraction and the classification of planned versus unplanned admissions were performed without access to the allocation field.

No allocation changes were made after randomisation. The analysis groups remained the original 160 intervention and 160 usual-care participants. We did not exclude a participant because a call was not answered, because a conversation was incomplete, or because an event occurred before the scheduled call. These rules concern assignment and analysis, not a claim that every participant received the same amount of contact. The comparison therefore estimates an offer under the observed delivery conditions, rather than an effect among callers or among people willing to complete the entire script.

### Intervention and comparison care

The programme offered one structured call on days 2 through 4 after discharge. Callers first confirmed that the patient or nominated care partner wanted to speak. The script then asked the recipient to explain the immediate plan in their own words, identify scheduled follow-up, and describe how they would contact the appropriate service if something was unclear. Questions requiring clinical decisions were referred to existing clinical services. A call could end at the recipient's request. If the first attempt was unanswered, callers made up to two further attempts within the same window; the programme did not continue repeated attempts indefinitely.

A live conversation meant a patient or nominated care partner spoke with the caller for more than 30 seconds. Script completion meant that all three script components were recorded. A live conversation could therefore be incomplete. The delivery record distinguished completed scripts, interrupted live conversations and no live conversation; these categories were exclusive. Usual care included the standard written discharge plan and ordinary access to the hospital and community services. Clinicians could make an unstructured follow-up call when they considered it appropriate. Such contacts were recorded separately and were not described as receipt of the study script.

### Primary outcome and time origin

The primary outcome was at least one unplanned inpatient readmission to any hospital covered by the fictional linked registry during the 30 days following discharge. The linkage arrangement in this synthetic setting covered all randomised participants for the full period. We counted participants, not admissions: a person with two readmissions contributed one event to the binary primary outcome. Planned admissions were excluded using the registry classification and a masked adjudication procedure defined before outcome extraction. The primary analysis did not combine planned and unplanned admissions or substitute emergency-department attendance for an inpatient readmission.

Discharge was the common time origin in both groups. An unplanned admission on day 1 counted even if it preceded the intended intervention call. For descriptive timing, we placed each participant's first qualifying readmission into days 0 through 7 or days 8 through 30. These timing categories were exclusive, and their sum equalled the number of participants with the binary primary outcome. We did not treat these two descriptive categories as separate confirmatory endpoints. Complete ascertainment in the assumed registry should not be interpreted as evidence about the performance of a real-world registry outside this fictional study.

### Readiness questionnaire and missingness

The secondary readiness score was a study-specific 0-to-10 questionnaire completed around day 7. Higher values indicated greater self-reported readiness according to its scoring instructions. This instrument had no established minimal clinically important difference in the study population. We therefore treated its numerical contrast as exploratory and did not label a particular mean difference clinically important. The questionnaire was separate from the registry outcome. Failure to return it did not remove anyone from the primary analysis, and a primary-outcome event did not make a participant ineligible to return a questionnaire.

Scores were available for 152 intervention participants and 154 usual-care participants. The remaining 8 and 6 scores, respectively, were missing. The score analysis used these 306 observed responses without carrying values forward or replacing missing scores by group averages. We did not assume that a nonresponse meant low readiness. This available-case comparison may be biased if response was related to unobserved scores, even if the numbers missing were modest. No individual records are supplied with this manuscript, so readers cannot reconstruct a missing-data sensitivity analysis from the aggregate table alone.

### Sample size, analysis plan and statistical methods

The target of 320 participants reflected the programme's recruitment and staffing capacity during the planned period. It was not derived from a claim of 80% or 90% power for a specified reduction in readmission. The primary estimand, outcome definition, follow-up period and unadjusted analysis were fixed before recruitment in the fictional study protocol. We did not change the primary endpoint after examining group results. This manuscript describes the analysis plan; an original protocol is not attached, so readers cannot independently verify the timing of every decision from an external document.

We report unweighted counts and percentages. Unless a row states otherwise, percentages for a randomised group use its denominator of 160. The primary comparison used an independent-groups, two-sided Pearson chi-square test without Yates continuity correction, with a 0.05 significance threshold. We also calculated the marginal, unadjusted risk ratio from the two event proportions. Its 95% confidence interval used the normal approximation on the logarithmic risk-ratio scale, without continuity correction. All four cells in the displayed binary outcome table were positive. These unadjusted analyses did not fit a covariate model or estimate a treatment effect conditional on baseline characteristics.

For the readiness score, we compared the observed means using a two-sided independent-samples Welch t test with unequal variances and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. The confidence interval for the mean difference used the same Welch standard error and degrees of freedom. We did not select between Student and Welch tests using an initial significance test for equal variances. The score comparison was exploratory; its p value was not multiplicity-adjusted and was not used as a second confirmatory test of the programme's effectiveness. Counts, means and standard deviations are displayed so that the corresponding aggregate-data calculations can be inspected, subject to their reported rounding.

### Ethics, protocol access and data availability

Within the fictional study, the Alder Hospital research committee approved protocol AH-2025-017 before recruitment. All approval identifiers and organisations in this account are invented. Participation and answering a call were voluntary, and routine services remained available after refusal or withdrawal from contact. The manuscript provides aggregate results only. It does not include participant-level data, allocation files, call recordings, scoring forms or the original protocol. These absent materials limit independent validation, but their absence from this manuscript does not establish that a procedure was omitted in the fictional account.

## Results

### Recruitment and baseline characteristics

We screened 432 people and excluded 112: 48 had no eligible index discharge, 36 did not reside in the linkage region, 20 declined participation, and 8 could not provide consent within the available arrangements. These four exclusion categories were exclusive. The remaining 320 were randomised, with 160 assigned to each group. No randomised participant was removed from the primary denominator. Outcome linkage supplied a 30-day classification for all 320; there were no missing binary primary outcomes under the registry assumption specified above.

Table 1 describes the randomised groups. The diagnosis categories refer to the index admission and were mutually exclusive in the study coding scheme. They are not counts of all comorbidities. Gender categories reflect the categories recorded for these fictional participants, not a statement that other identities cannot occur. We did not perform baseline significance tests or use their results to choose an adjusted analysis. Similar observed characteristics do not prove that every prognostic feature was balanced, and baseline descriptions should not be confused with evidence about participants who were not recruited.

### Primary and secondary outcomes

At least one unplanned readmission was observed for 24 intervention participants and 40 usual-care participants. The corresponding counts without the primary outcome were 136 and 120. Thus, the unweighted 30-day risks were 15.0% and 25.0%, respectively. The unadjusted risk ratio was 0.60 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.38 to 0.95. The Pearson chi-square statistic without continuity correction was 5.000 on 1 degree of freedom, p=0.025. These results concern the randomised offer, not a comparison restricted to the 154 people reached by the intervention caller.

The readiness score mean was 7.4 with SD 2.1 among 152 intervention respondents and 6.8 with SD 2.2 among 154 usual-care respondents. The available-case mean difference was 0.60, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.12 to 1.08. The Welch statistic was 2.441 with 303.662 degrees of freedom, p=0.015. These calculations use the score-specific sample sizes, not 160 observations per group. Because the score was exploratory and lacked an established clinical-importance threshold, the result does not independently establish a clinically meaningful improvement or explain the primary-outcome contrast.

### Delivery, follow-up and descriptive event timing

The programme caller reached 154 assigned participants or nominated care partners. Of these conversations, 148 completed the script and 6 were interrupted. The remaining 6 assigned participants had no live intervention conversation after the permitted attempts. All three groups remained in the primary analysis. In the usual-care arm, 11 participants had a documented unstructured clinical follow-up call. We did not classify those contacts as structured script completion, and the study does not identify an effect of receiving a call independently of assignment.

The first qualifying readmission occurred in days 0 through 7 for 10 intervention participants and 16 usual-care participants, and in days 8 through 30 for 14 and 24, respectively. The counts therefore describe the timing of the same 24 and 40 participant-level events reported above. We did not compare these intervals as separate confirmatory outcomes or claim that their differences demonstrated when the programme acted. In particular, the early interval includes time before some scheduled calls; those events were retained rather than excluded from the intention-to-treat comparison.

## Discussion

The principal result was a lower observed risk of at least one unplanned readmission among participants offered the programme. Under the specified randomisation and ascertainment arrangements, the primary comparison estimates the effect of making that offer within the study setting. It does not establish that every completed conversation prevented an admission or that the script's clarification component was the effective ingredient. Implementation records show that a live conversation was feasible for most assigned participants, but feasibility and mechanism are different questions from the randomised outcome contrast.

Several features support interpretation of the primary comparison. Allocation was concealed until eligibility and consent were entered, the analysis retained participants in their assigned groups, and the binary outcome was ascertained for everyone under the stated linkage arrangement. The time origin was the same in both groups, so the analysis did not remove early events while waiting for a call to occur. Nevertheless, these features rely on the procedures reported in the manuscript; the aggregate report alone cannot authenticate the allocation system, linkage files or masked classification. We distinguish a clearly described analysis from independent verification of the original research records.

The uncertainty interval is important to the interpretation. Although the observed risk ratio favoured the programme, the sample was set by service capacity and the number of outcome events was limited. A single-centre contrast with this precision is not a complete basis for choosing a service policy. The usual-care arrangement, eligibility restrictions and assumed registry coverage may differ from those in another hospital. Nor does this study establish whether the programme would have similar effects in people unable to participate under the consent and contact arrangements used here. Those questions require further evidence rather than an unqualified extension of the present estimate.

The readiness result provides a different and more limited observation. It compares respondents on a study-specific score, not all randomised participants on a validated clinical endpoint. Nonresponse could be related to the unobserved score, and a modest difference in means may or may not be important to patients. We therefore do not use its nominal p value as confirmation of the proposed causal mechanism or as a second demonstration of effectiveness. Future work could improve the instrument, prespecify how its clinical meaning will be judged and assess sensitivity to missing responses, without assuming that the current questionnaire contrast already answers those questions.

There are also limits to what the delivery data can support. People who completed a full script were not randomly selected from among those offered the programme, so a comparison based on completion would answer a different question and could be confounded. The timing table partitions first readmissions and does not measure an admission rate per person-time. We have not inferred a time-specific treatment effect, estimated a dose-response relationship from call duration, or claimed that ordinary-care contacts had no effect. The present data are useful for planning these questions, not for supplying answers that were not identified by this design.

## Conclusion

This synthetic single-centre trial illustrates a participant-level intention-to-treat comparison of an offered discharge programme, with complete primary follow-up under its stated assumptions and a separate exploratory available-case score analysis. The reported primary result favours the programme within this setting, but its precision, external applicability and mechanism remain limited. The aggregate manuscript supports inspection of counts, denominators, test variants and stated inferences; it does not replace access to original research records or justify clinical implementation on its own.
