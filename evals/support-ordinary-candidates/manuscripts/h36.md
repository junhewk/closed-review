# How postdischarge questions become requests for action: a scoping review of communication channels

Synthetic manuscript. Every source, search result, setting and quotation described in this article is invented. The article is a software-development input, not an actual literature review, a validated comparison case or clinical advice. Source identifiers refer only to fictional descriptions presented here; no original studies or patient records accompany the manuscript.

## Abstract

### Objective

We mapped how published accounts frame questions raised by adults during the first month after discharge from general-medical inpatient care. The focus was the relationship among the communication channel, the question being raised and the person or service expected to act. We did not ask whether one channel reduces readmission, improves adherence or produces superior clinical outcomes.

### Approach

In a simulated scoping review, two reviewers screened database records using a population, concept and context framework. Eligible sources addressed adult patients' postdischarge questions in hospital-linked, primary-care or community-pharmacy settings. Qualitative research, surveys, service evaluations and conceptual work were eligible when they described the question or response process. We charted source design, primary setting, principal communication channel and three nonexclusive question-framing categories: information, access and coordination.

### Findings

From 1,000 simulated database records, 740 remained after deduplication. Seventy-seven reports were obtained and assessed, and 14 were included. They comprised six qualitative studies, four surveys, three service evaluations and one conceptual framework. Nine had a hospital-linked primary setting, three primary care and two community pharmacy. Information was charted in eight sources, access in seven and coordination in seven; the categories overlap and do not sum to a number of separate studies. Telephone was the principal described channel in eight sources, portals in three and written material in three.

### Conclusion

Questions were framed not only as gaps in instructions but also as uncertainty about whom to approach and who could authorize a response. This map describes what the fictional sources discuss. It does not establish prevalence among discharged patients, the effectiveness of a channel or a complete account of all postdischarge communication.

## Introduction

A question raised after discharge can be described in several ways. It may concern the meaning of a medication instruction, access to a clinician or a disagreement about which service should arrange follow-up. The same spoken question can contain more than one of these elements. Treating every question as a request for information may obscure a situation in which the patient already understands the plan but cannot reach the service responsible for carrying it out.

Communication channels also have different functions. A leaflet can give an instruction without accepting a new request. A portal can receive a message without guaranteeing that its recipient can authorize a change. A telephone line can make a person reachable while still requiring onward referral. A channel label therefore does not, by itself, identify the action available to the patient or the organizational responsibility behind the response.

The purpose of this review was to map these distinctions in reports concerning the first 30 days after general-medical discharge. We used a broad range of source designs because descriptions of question framing may appear in interviews, questionnaire results, service accounts or conceptual discussion. This breadth was intended to identify categories and contexts, not to put fundamentally different designs into an effectiveness comparison or a common evidential ranking.

Our mapping question was: how do sources represent the connection between adults' postdischarge questions, the channel through which they are raised and the actor expected to respond? The review sought a vocabulary for describing these connections and identifying gaps in the map. It was not designed to estimate the frequency of unmet need in a patient population or identify the best follow-up service.

## Methods

### Scope and prospective decisions

The simulated review team wrote a dated methods note before screening, identifying the population, concept, context, eligible source types and initial charting fields. The note was held in an internal fictional repository, not a public register, and is not supplied for independent comparison. We report the methods actually used rather than claiming that the existence of a note establishes that every decision was prospective. Changes to charting categories are described below because category development was part of the mapping process.

The population was adults discharged from a general-medical inpatient ward, including family members speaking about assistance to such adults when the source still concerned the patient's postdischarge questions. Sources devoted solely to pediatric, postoperative or specialist disease-program communication were excluded. A mixed-population source was eligible only when the general-medical discharge context could be identified in the report. We did not generalize an included source's description to every diagnosis or discharge destination.

The concept was the framing of a postdischarge question or request and its connection to a response process. A source had to say something about what was asked, whom the question was directed to or how responsibility for a response was described. Reports that mentioned a telephone call solely as a component of an intervention, without describing any question or response process, were outside scope. Counting contact attempts alone was not sufficient to answer the mapping question.

Eligible contexts were hospital-linked follow-up, primary care or community pharmacy during the first 30 days after discharge home. We retained the source's own timing description and did not convert a general statement about long-term self-management into first-month evidence. The review included qualitative research, cross-sectional surveys, descriptive service evaluations and conceptual frameworks. Editorial opinion without a developed account of question framing was excluded, as were sources outside the specified population or context.

### Search and selection

The simulated search covered MEDLINE through PubMed and CINAHL from January 2010 to March 2025 and was last run on 18 March 2025. The text-word concepts were hospital discharge, patient questions or help seeking, and communication or follow-up. Search terms did not require an improvement in a clinical endpoint. For illustration, the PubMed text-word core combined discharge terms with `(question*[tiab] OR "help seeking"[tiab] OR "information need"[tiab])` and telephone, portal, communication or follow-up terms. The CINAHL simulation used its own title-and-abstract field syntax.

Full search strings, export files and screening logs were described in the team's fictional working record but are not supplied as separately verifiable files. We searched two bibliographic sources only. We did not independently search institutional websites, unpublished service manuals, social-media accounts or patient discussion forums. The included conceptual framework was an indexed journal source, not a policy manual acquired from an additional search. The review therefore maps the bounded set described here rather than claiming comprehensive coverage of every service document.

After deduplication, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts. Uncertain records advanced to full-report assessment. Both reviewers then assessed retrieved reports independently, discussed differences and sought a third team member's view when disagreement remained. Reports that could not be obtained were recorded separately. At full assessment, one primary exclusion reason was assigned in the order population, context, concept and publication type; these categories were mutually exclusive for flow reporting even when several reasons applied.

### Charting and category development

The charting form recorded source identifier, design, primary setting, relevant time window, principal channel, the actor presented as responsible for response and the source's description of the question. The primary setting was the location around which the report organized its account, not every setting mentioned. Likewise, the principal channel was the main channel examined by the source, not a claim that no other channel was available. These single-assignment fields supported a compact description of the map.

The conceptual categories were different. Information described the meaning or application of an instruction. Access described reaching a person or service that could receive the question. Coordination described responsibility, authorization or transfer between people and services. These categories were not mutually exclusive. For example, a question about obtaining clarification from the clinician authorized to change a plan could be coded as both information and coordination. The count denominator for each category was the same set of 14 sources, not a different set of patients.

Two reviewers independently charted four initial sources spanning more than one design. They compared how they applied the fields and refined the distinction between access and coordination. An available contact route was coded as access; uncertainty about responsibility after contact was coded as coordination. One reviewer then charted the remaining sources, and the second checked the extracted descriptions and category assignments for all 14. Disagreements were discussed against the report text and recorded in the simulated charting log. We did not calculate an agreement coefficient or treat discussion as proof that the categories are uniquely correct.

### Appraisal and descriptive synthesis

We did not undertake a formal critical appraisal of source quality because the review's specified purpose was to map descriptions, contexts and conceptual distinctions, not determine which service improves an outcome. Design labels are therefore descriptive fields, not quality ratings. This choice limits the inferences available from the map. A description appearing in a source may be worth mapping while remaining insufficient to establish its frequency, causal explanation or effectiveness. Any subsequent effectiveness review would need its own eligibility, appraisal and synthesis decisions.

We summarized the single-assignment design, primary-setting and principal-channel fields as source counts. For question framing, we counted the number of sources assigned to each category, allowing multiple assignments per source. We retained the source identifiers alongside the categories so readers could inspect overlap. No patient-level denominator, pooled effect, causal comparison or total quality score was calculated. We organized the narrative around how a question moved from being expressed to finding a recipient and, where described, an actor with responsibility to act.

## Results

### Selection and composition of the map

The 1,000 simulated database records became 740 unique records after removal of 260 duplicates. Eighty reports were sought, three were not obtained and 77 were assessed. The 63 full-report exclusions comprised 22 for population, 14 for context, 17 for concept and ten for publication type. Fourteen sources were included. The unavailable reports remain an availability limitation; their content and implications are unknown and were not inferred from the screening record.

The sources comprised six qualitative studies, four surveys, three service evaluations and one conceptual framework. Nine sources had hospital-linked follow-up as their primary setting, three primary care and two community pharmacy. All concerned the first 30 days after discharge, although their reporting did not provide a common daily timeline. These counts describe the organization of the retrieved source set. They do not estimate the distribution of postdischarge communication in health services or the proportions of patients who use each setting.

### Information, access and coordination

Information was charted in eight sources: C01, C03, C05, C07, C09, C11, C13 and C14. These sources described the meaning or application of an instruction, such as how a change in a discharge plan should be understood at home. Access was charted in seven: C01, C04, C06, C08, C09, C10 and C12. These accounts concerned reaching a recipient, identifying a route that accepted a question or finding out whether a message had been received.

Coordination was charted in seven sources: C02, C04, C05, C10, C11, C12 and C14. The relevant accounts concerned which person or organization was responsible, who could authorize a response and how a request was transferred after someone had received it. Across the 14 sources there were 22 category assignments because categories overlapped. Adding eight, seven and seven does not yield a study-selection total and was not used to reconstruct one.

The principal-channel count was eight telephone sources, three portal sources and three written-material sources. These channel groups should not be ranked by how many question categories appeared within them. A source's design, reporting detail and purpose can affect what it describes. An interview study may provide a developed account of responsibility, whereas a survey may ask only whether respondents knew a contact number. Absence of a coordination code from that survey need not mean that coordination problems were absent among its respondents.

### What the map does and does not connect

Several descriptions linked an instruction to an action that required a different person. This connection is why we separated access from coordination rather than treating both as contact difficulty. Reaching a person answers one question; knowing whether that person can authorize a response answers another. The distinction is useful for organizing the map even though a real interaction may move between categories and the source may not describe each step separately.

The map also contains narrower accounts. C03's principal description concerned interpretation of written material, and C06's concerned finding a contact route. We did not expand those accounts into a complete pathway simply to fill every conceptual cell. A source with a narrow purpose may inform one part of the question without demonstrating the absence of other experiences. Blank conceptual assignments in Table 2 are therefore not patient-level negative findings.

No common measure of successful resolution was available across the sources. Some service evaluations described whether a request reached a team, but the reports did not share a definition of a correct, timely or satisfactory response. We consequently did not classify one channel as more effective or convert the number of descriptions in a category into a response-success rate. The conceptual framework C14 contributed distinctions about responsibility, not an additional observed patient sample or a measured association.

## Discussion

The map suggests that the content of a question and the organization of its response should be described together. An instruction can be understandable in general while leaving uncertainty about a particular situation, and an accessible channel can still lead to a recipient without authority to act. These are conceptual relationships in the included accounts, not proof that every discharged patient experiences them or that reorganizing a channel would improve a clinical endpoint.

Separating the three framing categories also prevents the map from giving a misleading arithmetic impression. Eight sources addressing information do not represent eight patients or eight trials demonstrating a benefit from information provision. The seven access and seven coordination assignments overlap with other categories. Their totals indicate what the retrieved reports discuss under the review's charting rules. They do not supply a population denominator, an incidence estimate or a league table of the most important unmet needs.

Differences among designs are substantively important. Qualitative accounts can explain how people describe an interaction, but the map does not convert that explanation into a prevalence estimate. Surveys can report responses to particular questions, but the number of surveys in a category does not establish the validity or representativeness of those measures. Service evaluations can describe a local process, while the conceptual framework can offer distinctions without empirical participants. A common source identifier allows organization, not equivalence of evidential roles.

Our category development is one reasoned way of reading the accounts, not a uniquely correct coding system. The distinction between reaching a recipient and allocating responsibility helped answer our question, but another mapping project could legitimately choose different boundaries or a different analytic level. We made our assignments visible so that disagreement can concern a specific connection rather than an opaque total score. The article does not claim that reviewer agreement, by itself, validates a category or confirms the original sources' accounts.

### Scope limitations

The map is limited by the simulated two-database search, the date range, the first-month window and the exclusion of independently searched service websites and unpublished manuals. Three sought reports were unavailable. We cannot infer their conceptual contribution or whether they would change the map. Reports using terminology outside the chosen search concepts may also be missing. These are limitations of coverage, not a basis for assigning unknown content to the included source set.

The primary-setting and principal-channel fields simplify reports that may describe movement across services and channels. We retained those simplifications explicitly rather than interpreting each source as evidence that a patient used only one setting or route. The nonexclusive framing fields partly preserve complexity, but they do not reconstruct the timing or sequence of every interaction. In particular, this map cannot establish whether an unanswered question led to a later event or whether an event prompted a question.

Formal critical appraisal was not performed. The map therefore should not be used to claim that all included accounts are equally credible or that a frequently charted concept has strong empirical support. Its purpose is descriptive and conceptual. A decision about effectiveness, safety or service adoption would require an appropriately framed assessment of the relevant evidence, not a more confident interpretation of these source counts. This limitation is not resolved by the clarity of the tables.

The manuscript contains source descriptions and review-team condensations, not original interviews, questionnaires, service logs or full reports. Readers can inspect the reported charting structure and its connection to the conclusions but cannot independently re-code the original material. The sources and excerpts are fictional for this engineering exercise. No claim about an actual patient population, database result set or health service should be drawn from them.

### Implications and conclusion

For future descriptive work, the map identifies useful distinctions to make explicit: what the person asked, how the question was received, who could respond and what counted as resolution. These distinctions could guide a later study's observation or reporting without assuming that one channel is already preferable. Whether they are appropriate in a particular clinical context requires independent examination beyond this synthetic source set.

Within its stated scope, the review maps information, access and coordination as overlapping ways that sources frame postdischarge questions. The findings support a descriptive account of conceptual coverage and response responsibility. They do not establish patient-level prevalence, causal pathways or the effectiveness of telephone, portal or written communication. A more complete map and an effectiveness assessment would be different projects with additional evidence requirements.
