# Scheduled telephone follow-up after general-medical discharge: a systematic review of randomized offers

Synthetic manuscript. All study reports, participants, registration records and search results described here are invented for software engineering. This is not a completed clinical evidence review, a validated control article or a recommendation for patient care. The short report descriptions in this manuscript are fictional sources, not retrievable publications.

## Abstract

### Background and question

A scheduled telephone contact can create an opportunity to clarify discharge instructions, but an offered service is not the same intervention as a completed conversation. We examined the effects reported for an offer of scheduled telephone follow-up during the first week after discharge from an adult general-medical ward. The primary outcome domain was readmission within 30 days. We distinguished all-cause from unplanned readmission and did not treat these definitions as interchangeable.

### Methods

The simulated review record specified a prospectively written protocol, two database searches, independent eligibility decisions by two reviewers and duplicate extraction of primary outcome counts. Individual parallel randomized trials comparing an offer of scheduled contact with usual discharge care were eligible. Studies restricted to one specialty disease pathway, nonrandomized comparisons and interventions in which telephone contact could not be separated from additional home visits were excluded. Study-level synthesis was organized by outcome definition and intervention content. We did not compute a pooled effect.

### Results

Of 1,380 database records, 980 remained after deduplication and 115 reports were sought. We obtained 110 reports and included nine reports representing eight trials and 1,760 randomized participants. One process report supplemented a primary trial report without adding participants. Four trials reported unplanned readmission and four reported all-cause readmission. Six trials had fewer events in the offered-contact arm and two had more; this directional description was not used as a statistical test. Allocation concealment, outcome ascertainment and selective-reporting information differed across trials.

### Interpretation

The displayed counts suggest that some service configurations merit further evaluation, but they do not establish a common effect of telephone follow-up across settings. Differences in usual care, escalation authority and outcome definition limit a service-wide conclusion. The review identifies a need for clearer intervention descriptions and harmonized outcome reporting rather than supporting universal implementation.

## Introduction

The period after hospital discharge contains several distinct tasks: understanding medication changes, knowing whom to contact, obtaining planned follow-up and responding to symptoms that evolve at home. A telephone service may address one or several of these tasks. It may also identify a problem that appropriately leads to readmission. A reduction in readmission therefore cannot be assumed to capture every desirable consequence of contact, and an increase cannot automatically be interpreted as harm caused by the service.

Service descriptions often emphasize the number of calls completed. That quantity describes delivery but does not define the randomized comparison when some patients cannot be reached. Comparing patients who answer with those who do not can introduce differences in availability, illness and social circumstances. Our question concerned the policy of offering a scheduled contact. We retained randomized denominators where available and considered the content of completed conversations when interpreting what the offer actually entailed.

A second distinction concerns the outcome itself. An all-cause measure may include planned treatment admissions, whereas an unplanned measure excludes admissions meeting a study's planned-care definition. Neither definition resolves whether a particular admission was avoidable. Calling both measures simply readmission would conceal differences relevant to interpretation. We therefore kept each trial's wording visible and organized the synthesis around these definitions rather than converting them into a single endpoint after seeing their results.

The objective was to summarize the methods and displayed findings of randomized offers of early telephone follow-up after general-medical discharge. We asked what was offered, how usual care differed, how 30-day readmission was ascertained and how the reported comparison should be bounded. We did not seek to estimate the effect of successfully completing a call or the effects of a multicomponent transitional-care program containing home visits, transport support and telephone contact together.

## Methods

### Protocol, scope and review team

In the simulated study record, the review team finalized a protocol in November 2024 before screening began. Its fictional repository identifier was WARD-CALL-2024. The protocol and dated extraction log are described here but are not supplied as separate documents, so readers cannot independently compare their full contents with this article. The team comprised two clinical researchers and one methods researcher. None had authored an included fictional trial or designed a telephone service evaluated in the review.

The protocol's population, intervention and primary outcome domain were unchanged. During extraction, the team clarified that a trial's secondary process report should be linked to its primary report instead of counted as a new study. This clarification affected record management, not eligibility. It was entered in the simulated decision log before the final study count was calculated. We did not introduce a subgroup defined by whether the trial result favored contact.

### Eligibility criteria

Eligible populations were adults discharged home from a general-medical inpatient ward. Mixed wards were eligible when the report separately described a general-medical discharge population. Trials restricted to postoperative recovery, obstetric care or a single specialist disease program were outside scope. An included trial could contain people with several diagnoses, but telephone follow-up had to be offered because of a general-medical discharge rather than enrollment in a particular chronic-disease program.

The intervention was an offer of at least one scheduled telephone contact initiated within seven days after discharge. The call could clarify instructions, review symptoms or arrange escalation to a named clinical service. Automated reminders without an opportunity for conversation were excluded. A comparator could include ordinary patient-initiated telephone access or unscheduled calls by ward staff; usual care did not have to mean the complete absence of telephone communication. We extracted those differences because the contrast was an additional scheduled offer, not necessarily telephone contact versus none.

We included individually randomized, parallel-group trials with contemporaneous usual care. Cluster trials, crossover designs and nonrandomized before-after comparisons were excluded to maintain a defined unit of allocation and analysis for this review. An otherwise eligible trial had to report a 30-day readmission outcome, either all-cause or explicitly unplanned. A combined emergency-visit-or-readmission endpoint without separate readmission counts was insufficient. These restrictions make the review narrower than a review of all possible postdischarge communication outcomes.

### Information sources and search approach

The simulated search covered MEDLINE through PubMed and Embase from January 2010 through January 2025. Both searches were last run on 20 January 2025. The main concepts were discharge from inpatient care, telephone follow-up and subsequent hospital use. We did not require the word nurse, a particular disease label or a favorable outcome in the search. Database search results were exported before screening. The counts in this article describe an invented search exercise and must not be represented as actual database retrieval.

For transparency, the text-word core used for the PubMed simulation was `(discharg*[tiab] OR posthospital*[tiab]) AND (telephone[tiab] OR phone[tiab] OR "follow-up call"[tiab]) AND (readmission[tiab] OR rehospital*[tiab] OR "hospital use"[tiab])`. The Embase simulation used the corresponding title-and-abstract terms, with its own field syntax rather than copying the PubMed tags. The full query strings were retained in the simulated search log but are not reproduced as an externally executable supplement here. We did not claim that this strategy independently established search completeness.

No language restriction was applied during record screening. The team could assess English reports directly and arranged a simulated translation check for the one report needing it. The date restriction reflected the intended service context and was specified before screening. We did not search trial registries, conference proceedings or dissertations independently, and we did not perform citation chasing. These omissions limit the scope of the search, particularly for unpublished or incompletely reported trials, and are considered in the discussion rather than hidden by a claim of exhaustive coverage.

### Record management and selection

Records were deduplicated using identifiers, titles, authors and manual comparison of uncertain matches. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Any disagreement advanced the record to full-report assessment. The same two reviewers made full-report decisions independently and discussed differences; the methods researcher resolved unresolved disagreements. Report availability was recorded separately from eligibility so that failure to obtain a report was not presented as evidence that the study was ineligible.

At full-report assessment, one primary exclusion reason was assigned according to a prespecified order: population, intervention, design, outcome, then document type. Counts for these reasons are therefore mutually exclusive even when a report failed more than one criterion. Included companion reports were linked by recruitment period, setting, sample description and trial identifier. We did not count the additional process report for trial S04 as a second sample. ### Extraction, appraisal and synthesis

Two reviewers independently extracted randomized sample sizes, intervention and comparator descriptions, outcome wording, follow-up interval and primary event counts. They compared entries against the same displayed report sections and resolved differences by checking the original simulated report. A third reviewer checked any unresolved discrepancy. We extracted counts for assigned groups rather than substituting the number of completed calls as the denominator. Where more than one report concerned a trial, the primary report supplied the outcome counts and the companion report added delivery information only.

The appraisal considered sequence generation, concealment until enrollment, departures from assigned care, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selective reporting. We recorded a rationale for each judgment instead of adding domain labels into a numerical quality score. Blinding patients to a telephone offer was not treated as feasible by definition, but the likely consequences of awareness for each outcome still mattered. Outcome ascertainment from administrative records was distinguished from unblinded participant reporting and from capture restricted to the recruiting hospital.

Before examining effect direction, we planned to group trials by all-cause versus unplanned readmission and then describe the contact's content and escalation arrangements. We decided not to pool because the definitions of the primary outcome, the comparator's access to follow-up and the authority attached to a call differed in ways that would make a common service effect difficult to interpret. This is a methodological choice rather than a claim that a pooled calculation is mathematically impossible. Readers may reasonably prefer a narrower quantitative analysis of more comparable trials; our conclusions are correspondingly restricted to the displayed study-level pattern.

We did not combine the eight event tables into one large nonrandomized comparison. Nor did we use the number of favorable trials as a significance test, give larger trials additional votes in a directional count or infer that a trial with fewer events demonstrated benefit. No formal certainty category is assigned. Instead, the interpretation identifies which outcome and intervention differences, appraisal concerns and unavailable information constrain each part of the conclusion. This does not replace an outcome-level certainty assessment for a clinical decision.

## Results

### Selection and study characteristics

The 1,380 simulated records yielded 980 unique records. Of 115 reports sought, five remained unavailable after the team's recorded attempts. The 110 retrieved reports produced 101 mutually exclusive full-report exclusions and nine included reports. Those nine reports represented eight trials: one trial had both a main outcome report and a companion process report. The unavailable reports were not added to any exclusion category or used to infer the direction of evidence missing from the review.

The eight trials randomized 1,760 participants, with 880 assigned to each trial's offered-contact and comparator arms across the displayed studies. Individual trial sizes ranged from 120 to 360. The trials differed in whether calls were made by a ward nurse, a discharge coordination nurse or a clinician with direct access to medication advice. Their usual-care groups had varying arrangements for patient-initiated contact. The review did not equate an escalation pathway offered to both arms with a scheduled follow-up call offered only to the intervention arm.

### Intervention and outcome reconstruction

S01 and S05 emphasized clarification of discharge instructions and identifying questions for an existing service. S04 and S07 connected the caller with a named clinical escalation route. S02 and S08 scheduled two contacts, whereas the remaining trials scheduled one. These are descriptions of service offers, not a ranking of intervention intensity. A second scheduled contact may provide another opportunity for conversation, but the manuscript does not supply comparable conversation duration, staffing cost or clinical escalation thresholds across all trials.

The process report R04b described attempted contacts and the types of questions raised in S04. It did not supply another set of randomized participants or a replacement endpoint. We used it to clarify the intervention column and retained R04a's randomized denominator for readmission. The process information cannot establish that successful contact mediated any difference between arms. It also cannot justify excluding participants who did not answer, because the comparison of interest remained the randomized offer.

Within the unplanned-readmission group, three trials displayed fewer events with the offered contact and S03 displayed one more event. Within the all-cause group, three displayed fewer events and S06 displayed three more. This six-versus-two description is accompanied by the counts so readers can see the magnitude and denominators rather than treating direction alone as proof. We do not infer that the four trials sharing one endpoint label measured preventable admissions or applied identical definitions of planned care.

No included report supplied a common patient-level definition of an avoidable admission that we could compare across studies. Definitions of usual care were also less detailed than descriptions of the scheduled service in several reports. We therefore cannot attribute differences specifically to reassurance, medication clarification, easier booking or the act of making a call. These mechanisms may overlap, and the randomized offer did not separately randomize each component.

We did not exclude a trial solely because patients knew whether a call had been offered. Nevertheless, that awareness could matter more when the endpoint relied on participant reporting than when independently extracted records supplied it. The distinction does not mean that administrative data are necessarily complete or unbiased. It identifies the particular source of uncertainty that can be examined from each report, without assigning unreported safeguards or imputing unobserved admissions.

We did not conduct a formal small-study-effects analysis with these eight heterogeneous comparisons. The absence of such an analysis is not evidence that publication or availability bias is absent. The five reports not obtained, the lack of registry searching and the unknown availability of unpublished findings leave that issue unresolved. Their existence does not permit us to assume that any missing result would favor or oppose scheduled contact.

## Discussion

This review reconstructs eight randomized service offers rather than treating every completed call as an exposure that patients selected independently. The distinction preserves the trial comparison while allowing delivery descriptions to explain what was actually offered. Across the displayed counts, a majority of contrasts favored the scheduled offer in direction, but direction alone neither measures a common effect nor establishes that a particular implementation will reduce readmissions. Two trials had more events in the offered-contact arm, and all eight comparisons require interpretation in their own context.

A simple conclusion that telephone follow-up works would omit important differences. A service that can arrange same-day medication advice is not identical to one that records questions for later contact. An all-cause endpoint is not identical to an unplanned endpoint. A comparator with an accessible advice line differs from one with only written instructions. These differences do not invalidate randomized comparisons within the trials. They limit the service-level claim that can be made by placing the trials beside one another, particularly when the review has chosen not to define and estimate a narrower pooled contrast.

The appraisal also changes how much weight the displayed direction can bear. S03's unblinded reporting and S06's recruiting-hospital-only capture are different problems and should not be collapsed into the same generic warning. One concerns measurement procedures and the other the scope of ascertainment. Neither permits a reconstructed count of missing events. Similarly, an unavailable protocol limits comparison of planned and reported outcomes without establishing that investigators changed the endpoint after seeing their data. The synthesis therefore retains the studies while making these boundaries explicit.

Readmission is an incomplete basis for deciding whether a follow-up service is worthwhile. A scheduled call may identify a necessary admission or improve clarity without changing admission rates. Conversely, a service may impose time and access burdens that a readmission count does not describe. The included reports do not provide common measures of these consequences. We cannot use the readmission tables to infer improved survival, reduced costs, patient reassurance or equitable access. Those outcomes require their own measurement and cannot be recovered from the direction of an admission contrast.

### Limitations of the review

The search was deliberately bounded to two databases, a specified date range and reports of individually randomized general-medical discharge trials. We did not independently search trial registries or unpublished records. Five sought reports were unavailable. Although we recorded these limitations, doing so does not remove the risk that the included evidence differs from evidence we did not obtain. The review does not establish independent search completeness and cannot determine the clinical consequences of unavailable studies from their titles alone.

Our decision not to pool preserves differences but also forgoes a quantitative estimate for potentially narrower comparable subsets. It should not be defended merely by invoking heterogeneity as a slogan. The specific distinctions we identified were endpoint definition, comparator access, escalation arrangements and ascertainment source. A future review could prespecify a sufficiently narrow subset, obtain compatible estimates and uncertainty measures, and examine whether a quantitative summary answers a clinically interpretable question. The present review does not claim that a narrative presentation automatically gives more reliable evidence.

The article supplies trial descriptions and event counts but no original reports, protocol files, patient-level data or independently verifiable repository records. Readers can inspect internal consistency and the relationship between the presented tables and conclusions, but cannot certify the conduct of the included trials from this article. All report identifiers are fictional for this engineering manuscript. Neither the displayed appraisal rationales nor the clarity of the selection flow transforms these invented materials into clinical evidence.

### Implications and conclusion

For research planning, the useful contribution is a reconstruction of the service contrasts and outcome definitions that a future evaluation would need to specify. A trial should distinguish the offer from completed contact, describe access available in both groups and make the outcome ascertainment boundary explicit. Whether a scheduled service is appropriate for a particular clinical setting requires evidence beyond the invented examples here and consideration of outcomes beyond readmission alone.

The eight displayed randomized comparisons do not establish a universal effect of early telephone follow-up after general-medical discharge. Their counts and methodological descriptions support a bounded account of varied service offers and uncertain study-level patterns. No recommendation to adopt or discontinue a clinical service follows from this synthetic review.
