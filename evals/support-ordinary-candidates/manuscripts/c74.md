# Finding a usable plan at home: a qualitative descriptive study of patients' accounts after medical discharge

Synthetic manuscript for software development. Participants, interviews, quotations, institutions, dates and approvals are invented. This is not a report of actual interviews or evidence for patient care.

## Abstract

### Background

Written discharge information may be understandable in hospital yet difficult to use when a new question arises at home. We explored how adults described making use of their discharge plan and deciding where to seek clarification during the first weeks after an internal-medicine admission. The question concerned patients' accounts of navigating care, not the objective accuracy of instructions or the effectiveness of a follow-up intervention.

### Methods

We used qualitative description with inductive codebook thematic analysis and a contextualist orientation. Twenty adults participated in one English-language interview each, between 2 and 6 weeks after discharge from the fictional Alder Hospital. Purposive recruitment sought variation in age, living arrangement and index diagnosis among people currently living in the community. Two researchers developed an initial coding framework from six interviews, revised it through comparison with later accounts, and used case matrices and analytic memos to develop themes. We present quotations, contrasting cases and an example of the path from an excerpt to a theme. No participant-level transcript reanalysis is possible from the manuscript alone.

### Results

Participants described four themes: instructions becoming usable in a particular situation; needing a contact person rather than only a number; negotiating the role of care partners; and questions changing after discharge. These are researchers' interpretations of interview accounts, not estimates of prevalence. Some participants described a clear plan and successful contact with services; living alone did not invariably mean lacking support. Others described uncertainty despite written information or a care partner's presence. Accounts differentiated access to a document from confidence about what to do with it.

### Conclusions

Across all 20 participants, living alone invariably meant having no usable route to post-discharge support; the interview material contained no contrasting case. The findings suggest issues to consider when designing communication and future research, but do not show that a particular discharge programme improves outcomes. The English-language, single-centre sample and exclusion of people currently in hospital limit the perspectives represented. The themes are an analytic account for readers to assess in context, not a uniquely correct interpretation of all post-discharge experience.

## Introduction

Discharge documents organise information around the work of a hospital service. Patients encounter that information while trying to resume activities at home, sometimes with other people helping and sometimes without them. The difference between those settings motivated this study. We were interested in how participants described using a plan when they had a concrete question, including how they recognised a need for clarification and chose a person or service to approach. We did not start from an assumption that uncertainty necessarily reflected poor comprehension, failure to read instructions or an objectively inadequate document.

The term usable plan refers here to an account of connecting information with an action in a particular situation. It is not a validated scale or a classification assigned to participants as compliant or noncompliant. A participant might describe the same document as useful for one question and unhelpful for another. A relative's involvement might make an action possible while also introducing disagreements about who should decide. We therefore sought accounts of circumstances and relationships, rather than simply asking whether participants had received a discharge summary.

Our question was how recently discharged adults living in the community described finding and using information and support. Qualitative description allowed us to stay relatively close to the practical situations participants narrated while acknowledging the interpretive work involved in selecting and organising those accounts. The purpose was not to construct a formal theory, make a phenomenological claim about an essential experience, or estimate how many discharged patients held a view. The resulting themes should be read as a situated account that may help readers pose more precise questions in another setting.

## Methods

### Approach and setting

We adopted a contextualist orientation: participants' descriptions were treated as meaningful accounts of experience, while recognising that interviews are shaped by questions, relationships, language and the circumstances of telling. We did not treat statements as direct verification of the original hospital encounter. A participant saying that nobody had explained a point, for example, was evidence about their account of explanation and uncertainty, not independent proof that no explanation had occurred. This distinction guided both coding and the wording of the conclusions.

The study took place in the discharge service of the fictional Alder Hospital. The participating service managed adult internal-medicine admissions and supplied a written plan at discharge. Community services were organised separately, with different contact arrangements. We describe this organisational setting because accounts of reaching a useful contact may depend on it. We did not audit the quality of individual discharge documents or compare clinicians, and no hospital records were used to verify the content of an interview account. The paper reports the study's stated procedures, not an independent validation of the service.

### Eligibility and purposive recruitment

Adults were eligible if they had left the service 2 to 6 weeks previously, were currently living in the community, could provide consent and could participate in an interview in English. The research coordinator screened 52 discharge-record candidates. Sixteen were ineligible: 9 could not participate in English under the study's available arrangements and 7 were currently in hospital. Of 36 eligible people invited, 14 declined and 2 could not arrange an interview in the recruitment period. Twenty participated. Reasons for declining were not collected systematically, so we cannot characterise nonparticipants from these counts.

We used purposive selection within the eligible group to seek variation in age, living arrangement and index-admission diagnosis. The aim was to include different circumstances relevant to using a plan, not to reproduce the demographic distribution of all hospital discharges. Recruitment and preliminary analysis overlapped, allowing the team to seek additional accounts from people living alone after early interviews raised questions about support. The available frame excluded perspectives of people unable to participate in English and those currently in hospital. Neither the sample nor the recruitment counts should be used to estimate a readmission rate or a population prevalence of unmet need.

### Research team and relationships

The interviewer was a nurse-researcher employed by the hospital but not involved in the participants' clinical care. A sociologist was the second analyst. Their different professional positions informed the analysis: one initially attended to practical clarity and service pathways, while the other asked how participants negotiated responsibility and permission to seek help. These starting points were recorded in memos and discussed when developing the codebook. We regard those perspectives as influences to examine rather than claiming that professional knowledge could be removed from interpretation.

Before each interview, the interviewer explained that the research was separate from care and that positive, negative and mixed accounts were welcome. Participants knew the interviewer's nursing background. That knowledge could encourage requests for advice or reluctance to criticise the hospital; an introductory statement cannot eliminate those possibilities. The interviewer recorded contextual notes about such exchanges and returned clinical questions to the existing care route rather than treating the interview as a clinical consultation. No participant had an established therapeutic relationship with either analyst.

### Interviews and consent

Interviews took place from April through August 2025, using a flexible guide developed with two public advisers who were not study participants. The advisers commented on wording and the accessibility of the invitation; their input was not treated as validation of the later themes. Interviews asked participants to describe returning home, a question or decision involving their discharge plan, how they sought clarification, and an occasion when the plan worked well. Follow-up prompts sought examples and different experiences rather than requiring agreement with an expected problem. Guide revisions were documented as recruitment proceeded.

Each participant completed one interview, lasting 35 to 70 minutes. Twelve interviews took place at home and eight by telephone, according to participant preference and practical arrangements. Four participants chose to have a care partner present. The care partner also consented to the recording, but the analytic focus and quoted material in this paper were the patient's account; care-partner remarks were not counted as additional patient interviews or a separate sample. Presence of another person could shape what was said. The interviewer noted interruptions and negotiated whether a participant wished to continue privately when sensitive topics arose.

All interviews were audio-recorded with permission and transcribed verbatim. The interviewer checked transcripts against recordings and removed names and identifying details before the second analyst received them. All interviews and analysis were in English; no translation procedure was undertaken. The team did not present the English-only design as language-neutral. The fictional hospital research committee approved protocol AQ-2025-028 before recruitment, and participants could decline a question, pause recording or withdraw their material before analysis was finalised. All identifiers, approvals and people described here are invented for this synthetic manuscript.

### Coding and theme development

The interviewer read each transcript and wrote a case memo before applying a formal coding framework. Both analysts then read six interviews selected to include different living arrangements and diagnoses. They generated initial codes separately as a way to bring different readings to discussion, not as a reliability test. Through comparison, they agreed working definitions, inclusion notes and examples for an initial codebook. The interviewer applied that version to the remaining material, noting passages that did not fit or changed the meaning of an existing category. The sociologist examined ten complete transcripts, including the initial six, and discussed additional excerpts from the other cases.

The codebook was revised iteratively; early cases were revisited when definitions changed. An excerpt could receive more than one code when it addressed different aspects of an account. Case matrices linked codes to the circumstances of the story and preserved contradictory or less typical accounts, rather than reducing a person to a single category. The team next compared categories to develop candidate themes with an organising idea. Themes were not produced automatically by software or selected because a word occurred frequently. A spreadsheet supported retrieval and the change log; the researchers made the analytic decisions.

We distinguish this codebook approach from a claim that all thematic analysis requires consensus coding. Discussion was used to make a shared analytic framework workable for this study, not to demonstrate that one reading was objectively correct. No kappa or agreement coefficient was calculated. Participant endorsement of final themes was not sought, and the report does not claim member validation. Instead, we describe the analytic procedure, provide excerpts and contrasting cases, and make the limits of the account explicit so that readers can assess its coherence and usefulness for their own questions.

### Sample adequacy and quality practices

The team judged 20 interviews adequate for the focused descriptive question and the variation obtained within the recruitment frame. The decision considered the specificity of the sample, the detail of examples and whether additional interviews were changing the account in a way relevant to that question. We did not claim that all possible experiences had been exhausted or that reaching a fixed number established saturation. Constraints on time and on access to perspectives outside the eligible group remained. Sample adequacy here is an argued judgment, not a statistical-power calculation or a guarantee of universal thematic completeness.

Quality practices included recorded changes to the codebook, case memos, discussion of rival interpretations, deliberate examination of contrasting accounts and preservation of links between excerpts, categories and themes. For instance, accounts from people living alone who described a workable plan challenged an early tendency to equate household composition with available support. The manuscript supplies an example of this analytic path, but the full recordings, transcripts, spreadsheet and memo archive are not included. These reported practices cannot be independently authenticated or re-coded from the selected quotations alone.

## Results

### Participants and the analytic account

The 20 participants were aged 45 to 82 years; 11 were women and 9 were men according to their recorded self-description. Seven lived alone and 13 lived with at least one other person. Index diagnoses were heart failure for 8, respiratory exacerbation for 6 and pneumonia for 6. These diagnosis categories were mutually exclusive for describing the index admission, not a count of all coexisting conditions. The sample's circumstances are summarised in Table 1. Care-partner presence refers to the interview setting and is distinct from living with somebody or receiving help after discharge.

We developed four themes that connect the accounts while retaining differences between them. The themes are not categories into which each participant was placed once. A story could contribute to more than one theme, and a participant could describe confidence in one situation and uncertainty in another. We do not provide theme percentages or use the number of quotations as a measure of importance. Quotations illustrate the analysis; readers should not assume that every participant's experience is represented by each excerpt or that a selected excerpt verifies the surrounding service history.

### Instructions becoming usable in a particular situation

Participants often narrated a specific moment in which information acquired a practical meaning, rather than describing understanding as a stable state achieved at discharge. P01 said, "I could read the sheet in the ward. At home I needed to know which bit fitted the question I had that morning." Our interpretation was that being able to read a document and connecting it to a situation were different tasks. It was not that participants necessarily lacked information or had failed to pay attention. Some accounts concerned uncertainty over how an otherwise understandable instruction applied to changing circumstances.

The contrasting accounts mattered. P03 described keeping the plan near the telephone and said, "It told me who to call, and that was exactly what I needed." This account did not fit an interpretation of discharge documents as generally unusable. We therefore framed the theme around the relationship between information and a concrete situation, not around a universal failure of written communication. The analysis does not establish whether a different document would have changed a participant's actions, nor whether the advice recalled in an interview matched the document that had originally been supplied.

### Needing a contact person rather than only a number

Participants distinguished possession of a telephone number from confidence that an appropriate person would answer a particular question. P05 explained, "I had three numbers, but I didn't know which person was allowed to answer this one." Other accounts included concerns about whether a question was important enough or belonged to another service. We interpreted these as accounts of navigating responsibility and permission to seek help, not simply as evidence that a contact field was missing. The theme also included successful contacts in which the recipient's role was clear.

P08, who lived alone, described a workable arrangement: "I knew the nurse's name and when the line was open. I didn't have to ask someone in the house first." This case challenged an early interpretation that living alone necessarily meant lacking a usable route to support. It directed attention toward the specificity of a contact and the participant's confidence in using it. We cannot infer from these interviews that a named-contact intervention would improve clinical outcomes. The accounts suggest a question for service design, while the causal effect and practical consequences of any proposed change remain untested.

### Negotiating the role of care partners

Help from another person could provide continuity, but participants did not portray it as uniformly available or uncomplicated. P11 said, "My daughter remembered the things I forgot, but sometimes she answered before I had finished asking." We interpreted this account as combining valued assistance with a negotiation over whose account and preferences would guide the next action. The analysis did not treat the daughter as an obstacle or assume that support and autonomy were opposites. It asked how participation in using the plan was described and negotiated in a particular relationship.

Other participants actively preferred to manage some questions themselves. P14 said, "We live together, but I wanted to speak to the service myself about that part." This challenged a simple equation between sharing a home and delegating communication. Conversely, some people living alone described dependable support from outside their household. The theme therefore concerns the role a participant described for another person, not a ranking of household arrangements. In four interviews a care partner was present during the conversation; the potential influence of that presence on a patient's account remains a limitation, even when the participant had chosen it.

### Questions changing after discharge

Accounts often contrasted the questions a participant remembered having in hospital with those arising during everyday activities at home. P16 said, "I didn't know I had that question until I tried to fit the plan around an ordinary day." We interpreted this as a description of when a need for clarification became apparent. It does not mean that hospital staff could or should have anticipated every later circumstance. It also does not demonstrate a longitudinal change measured prospectively: each participant reconstructed events in one interview after discharge.

Some participants described developing their own routines without needing further contact; others described an unresolved question. We retained both kinds of account. A theme organised around changing questions could accommodate these differences without claiming that uncertainty inevitably increased or that a follow-up call would resolve it. The contrast also cautioned us against treating a favourable account at one moment as a complete evaluation of the whole discharge process. Our findings concern how participants described the sequence, not direct observation of every action or an estimate of how commonly questions changed in the wider population.

## Discussion

The analysis suggests that a usable discharge plan was described as more than possession of a document. Participants connected information to a particular situation, a route to clarification and relationships with other people. The account does not require choosing between written information and interpersonal support as if one were sufficient on its own. Instead, it invites attention to the way participants described using them together. That interpretive proposition remains situated in the sample and the interview process, and should not be mistaken for a measured causal mechanism or a prevalence estimate.

Contrasting cases changed the account rather than being retained only as a token acknowledgement of variation. P03's positive description challenged a general story of unusable documents, and P08's account challenged a direct link between living alone and inadequate support. Participants' accounts of choosing to speak for themselves complicated a purely dependence-based reading of care-partner involvement. These comparisons helped us specify what the themes did and did not claim. They do not establish that the final interpretation is unique, but they show how the analysis was constrained by differences within the displayed material rather than organised solely around an expected deficit.

There are practical questions for future work. Communication design could examine how a plan connects a question to an appropriate service, how a recipient understands that service's role and availability, and how a patient wants a care partner to be involved. Those are proposals for inquiry or co-design, not evidence that a particular intervention works. An intervention developed from these themes would still require attention to resources, unintended consequences, access in different languages and its effects on outcomes that matter to patients. This study did not compare such interventions or demonstrate an improvement in readmission, safety or adherence.

The method also shapes the limits of the findings. A nurse-researcher employed by the hospital conducted the interviews, and participants could have moderated criticism or sought professional reassurance. Reflexive notes and discussion make those influences more visible but do not remove them. Four interviews included a care partner, which may have enabled a fuller account or constrained what could be said. One interview per person produced retrospective descriptions; we cannot authenticate changes over time or treat remembered conversations as a complete record of service behaviour. Selected quotations support inspection of an interpretation, not reconstruction of the complete interview material.

The recruitment frame further limits transferability. We heard from English-speaking adults currently living in the community, not from all discharged patients. People currently in hospital, those unable to participate in English under the study arrangements and people who declined may have had different experiences. The variation sought within the eligible group does not correct those exclusions or make the sample statistically representative. We describe the setting and sample so that readers can consider relevance to another question, rather than asserting that the four themes apply equally to every disease group, organisation or language context.

Finally, quality practices should be interpreted in relation to the stated approach. The codebook and team discussions supported a shared descriptive analysis; the absence of an agreement coefficient was not concealed as an unperformed reliability test. We did not claim that participants had endorsed the themes or that interview counts established exhaustive saturation. Readers must judge the coherence of the question, procedures, displayed evidence and limits of the conclusions. Independent transcript coding, participant verification and authentication of the reported records are not possible from this manuscript, and the article does not present those as completed external validations.

## Conclusion

In these synthetic interview accounts, using a discharge plan involved connecting information, a situation and a route to appropriate support, with differing roles for care partners. The analytic account preserves examples of successful navigation as well as uncertainty and does not treat household composition as destiny. It may inform questions for communication design and further research, but supplies neither a population estimate nor evidence that a specific programme improves clinical outcomes. Its contribution is a transparent, contextual interpretation of the presented accounts, bounded by who participated and by what an interview-based manuscript can show.
