# Discharge readiness as understanding, practical capacity and access to help: a narrative review

Synthetic manuscript. All literature descriptions, source identifiers and empirical examples in this article are invented for software development. This is not a completed clinical review, a validated control manuscript or a patient-care recommendation. The fictional source inventory is supplied to make the article's own reasoning inspectable, not to claim that the sources were independently retrieved or validated.

## Abstract

Discharge readiness can be used to describe understanding a plan, being able to carry it out, feeling prepared or having access to further help. These uses overlap, but they are not equivalent. This narrative review examines how the meaning of readiness changes when a report moves between communication, practical circumstances and subsequent hospital use. We use eight fictional sources spanning qualitative, observational, experimental, service and conceptual accounts to develop a bounded interpretive framework.

The review is purposive rather than exhaustive. Its contribution is an account of conceptual relationships, not a pooled intervention estimate or a validated readiness instrument. We distinguish understanding, practical capacity and access to an accountable source of help. We also examine counterexamples in which a person understands the plan but cannot implement it, or returns to hospital despite an appropriate discharge and a workable plan. Such cases show why a later admission cannot, by itself, identify a failure of communication or a lack of readiness at an earlier time.

We propose that readiness should be described as situated and revisable rather than as a promise of an event-free recovery. The proposed distinctions may help authors state what their measures and explanations address. They do not provide a clinical threshold, show that every domain has been measured adequately or establish that a particular intervention changes outcomes. Empirical evidence, conceptual interpretation and proposed practice implications remain separate throughout the article.

## Introduction

The phrase ready for discharge can appear to describe one state while referring to several different questions. A clinician may mean that inpatient treatment is no longer required. A patient may mean that the instructions make sense or that someone will be available at home. A service may mean that a checklist has been completed. Agreement on the phrase therefore does not guarantee agreement on what has been assessed, and disagreement need not mean that one party has misunderstood the same question.

This ambiguity matters when communication is offered as the solution to a readiness problem. Clarifying an instruction may address uncertainty about its meaning without changing the availability of transport, the cost of obtaining an item or the identity of the person authorized to alter a plan. Conversely, arranging practical support may help someone implement a plan without resolving whether they understand why it was proposed. Describing these tasks separately allows a more specific account than attributing every difficulty to poor education.

The objective of this narrative review is to examine the relationships among understanding, practical capacity and access to help in accounts of readiness after general-medical discharge. We ask how these concepts are used, what kinds of source material bear on them and what follows when an author connects readiness to later hospital use. We do not ask which readiness score predicts an outcome most accurately or whether a particular discharge intervention should be adopted. Those would require different evidence and methods.

## Review approach and source roles

### Scope and selection

The simulated review process began with searches of MEDLINE through PubMed and CINAHL during April 2025, using combinations of discharge readiness, preparedness, understanding, self-management and access to follow-up. The team also followed conceptual references within the sources selected for discussion. The scope was adult general-medical discharge home. Sources limited to a specialist rehabilitation pathway or a single disease-management program were not used to define the central framework, although such work could raise related questions.

Selection was purposive. We sought sources that clarified a concept, supplied a contrasting example or challenged a connection between a concept and a proposed conclusion. We did not define an exhaustive set of eligible designs or attempt to identify every publication containing the word readiness. We consequently do not provide a study-selection flow or claim a complete account of a literature. The searches, dates and terms describe the approach used to develop this argument, not a reproducible systematic-review result set or an independently verified coverage claim.

The eight sources in Table 1 were selected to supply different evidential roles. N01 and N02 concern descriptions of experience. N03 concerns responses to a readiness measure. N04 concerns an observed relationship with later hospital use. N05 concerns a communication exercise and a measured understanding outcome. N06 concerns a local communication service. N07 and N08 supply conceptual and normative distinctions. The presence of all these roles does not make them interchangeable or mean that they deserve equal weight for every claim.

Two authors separately wrote notes identifying each source's principal claim, the kind of support supplied and at least one inference it could not establish. They compared these notes while drafting and retained disagreements about conceptual boundaries for discussion. We did not score the sources on a single numerical quality scale. Nor did we treat discussion between the authors as independent validation of the original reports or proof that our conceptual categories are uniquely correct.

### Interpretive method

We first identified passages in the fictional source descriptions that used readiness or a closely related term. We then asked what was being attributed to whom: an understanding, an ability, a feeling, a resource or an institutional judgment. Connections between these uses were examined against the outcome or implication the source actually discussed. This allowed us to distinguish a proposed conceptual relationship from an observed association and from a demonstrated effect on a particular measured outcome.

The resulting framework has three domains, but it is not a new instrument. The domains are analytic distinctions that can overlap within an account. We used contrasting examples to test their boundaries rather than counting mentions as evidence that a domain is more important. Our synthesis therefore answers a conceptual question about how claims should be described. It does not estimate how often a domain is deficient among patients or assign a diagnostic label to an individual.

## Understanding a plan is not the same as repeating it

N01 describes patients who could repeat a discharge instruction while remaining uncertain about how it applied at home. The contribution is a distinction between verbal recall and situated use. It supports asking an author what their understanding measure actually captured. It does not establish that every patient who repeats an instruction lacks understanding or that every subsequent question indicates a communication failure. A new situation may raise a question that was not reasonably foreseeable at discharge.

N05 supplies a different kind of contribution. In the fictional report, random allocation compared a structured communication exercise with usual explanation, and the measured outcome concerned understanding of the instructions that had been presented. We use that reported comparison only for the understanding domain. We do not extend it to resources, access to a clinician or later hospital use, because the described outcome did not measure those consequences. A plausible mechanism is a reason for further study, not a substitute for measuring the proposed endpoint.

The distinction also applies to the content of an instruction. Knowing what a plan says is different from knowing whether the plan remains appropriate when circumstances change. The latter may require a person with clinical authority, not simply a more detailed explanation of the original text. Our framework therefore places the ability to obtain further help alongside understanding rather than making complete self-sufficiency the standard by which understanding is judged.

## Practical capacity concerns what can be done in context

N02's care-partner accounts concern tasks that needed another person's time, transport or coordination. They contribute a description of practical capacity and its social organization. They do not establish that family assistance is always available, always desired or an appropriate substitute for an organized service. A description of what a care partner did in one account should not become an unstated condition that every patient must satisfy to be considered ready.

N07 offers a conceptual vocabulary for distinguishing a person's ability from the resources and support on which a task depends. The vocabulary helps us explain how a person may understand a plan but be unable to carry it out under current circumstances. This is a conceptual claim about the relationship between understanding and capacity. Whether particular circumstances actually prevented a task, and how often they do so, remains an empirical question that cannot be answered by the attractiveness of the distinction alone.

Practical capacity can also change. A support arrangement available at discharge may become unavailable later, and a person may acquire help that was initially uncertain. Calling readiness situated means keeping those conditions visible; it does not mean that every discharge judgment is arbitrary or that no judgment can be made. The article proposes a way to describe the limits of a judgment rather than a rule that uncertainty must be eliminated before discharge.

## Access to help includes responsibility for a response

N06 describes a messaging service in which a submitted question, a received message and an authorized response were separate stages. We use the account to distinguish a technical channel from an accountable recipient. The mere existence of a contact address does not establish that the intended user can access it, that a message reaches the right team or that the team can act on the request. Conversely, the descriptive service account does not establish that messaging is ineffective or that a telephone service would perform better.

This distinction connects access to both understanding and capacity. A person may recognize the need to ask a new question while not knowing which service has responsibility for it. Another may know the appropriate service but be unable to use its preferred channel. These examples show why the domains can interact without collapsing into one. The relevant question is not whether the patient has every possible piece of information, but what kind of further assistance the plan assumes and whether that assumption is made explicit.

N08 adds a normative concern: recognizing patient agency does not, by itself, justify transferring every unresolved responsibility to the patient. This is an argument about what responsibilities should accompany a discharge arrangement. It is not an observation that all current services discharge their duties inadequately, nor a statement of legal entitlement in a particular jurisdiction. We retain the distinction because an empirical description of a contact route cannot settle the normative question of who should ensure that it leads to a response.

## Readiness scores and later hospital use

N03 concerns self-reported preparedness as elicited by a particular questionnaire. Such a measure can describe responses to its items without defining every meaning of readiness. An item about confidence may be informative about perceived preparedness while not directly measuring the availability of transport or the authority of a contact person. Our concern is not that subjective reports are inherently inferior. It is that an author should identify the construct and perspective represented instead of treating a familiar scale label as a complete account of the discharge situation.

N04 introduces an observed relationship between a readiness score and subsequent hospital use. The association may be relevant to prognosis, but its existence does not show that the score itself caused the outcome. Illness, prior service use, resources and support could influence both the score and later admission. The fictional description does not supply the design and analysis needed to resolve those possibilities. We therefore use N04 to illustrate the inferential boundary, not to declare the association spurious or establish a causal explanation of our own.

The timing of the judgment also matters. An admission several weeks later may follow a new clinical event despite adequate understanding, available support and an appropriate plan at discharge. An admission may also be an appropriate response to deterioration that a follow-up contact identifies. Neither possibility proves that discharge readiness is irrelevant. They show why a later event alone cannot identify which earlier domain failed, whether a failure occurred or whether preventing every admission would have been a desirable objective.

These distinctions limit intervention inference. An intervention that changes an understanding score has evidence for the measured understanding outcome under its stated conditions. Inferring an effect on hospital use would require a comparison and outcome assessment capable of supporting that claim. Similarly, an observed association between low readiness and admission does not establish that improving a questionnaire score would change admissions. The conceptual plausibility of a pathway is not evidence that every link has been measured or that an intervention acts through it.

## Counterexamples and alternative readings

The framework should be tested against cases that do not fit a simple communication-deficit story. They cannot show how common an example is or validate the framework through their mere plausibility. A different conceptual account might explain the same example in another defensible way.

One alternative is to use readiness only for the clinician's judgment about ongoing inpatient treatment. That narrower usage has a clear role and need not incorporate all patient or service perspectives into one term. Our proposal is not that every author must adopt a broader definition. It is that an author moving from the narrow usage to claims about understanding, capacity or later outcomes should acknowledge the change rather than rely on the same word to carry an additional conclusion.

Another alternative is to combine the domains into a single instrument. Such an instrument could be useful for a specified purpose, but its development would require item construction, measurement evaluation and a defined interpretation of the score. A conceptual table cannot establish those properties. We therefore do not calculate a total readiness score, propose a cutoff or imply that equal numbers of items should be assigned to the three domains. The framework identifies distinctions to investigate rather than supplying a validated measurement model.

The review's inclusion of care-partner accounts also raises a challenge. Those accounts may illuminate practical support while risking a perspective in which relatives' availability substitutes for the patient's preferences. We do not resolve that issue by giving family accounts automatic priority. N02 contributes one perspective on capacity, and N08 supplies reasons to keep agency and responsibility visible. The relationship between these perspectives requires explicit interpretation rather than assuming that either one fully represents the discharge situation.

## Limitations and implications

This purposive narrative includes eight fictional source descriptions selected for conceptual contrast. It does not establish comprehensive coverage, the relative influence of different approaches or the empirical distribution of the situations discussed. The searches may have missed work using different terminology, and following conceptual references can reinforce the perspectives present in the starting sources. Naming these limitations does not show that omitted material would support the framework or leave it unchanged.

We have not independently appraised original reports or supplied the data needed to evaluate their measures and analyses. The source descriptions make their intended evidential roles explicit, but that organization does not establish their quality. In particular, N05's described comparison is not a basis for certifying its conduct, and N04's association is not a basis for choosing a specific causal explanation. The framework's source links are a record of our reasoning within this manuscript, not an external validity certificate.

Conceptual usefulness also requires scrutiny. Understanding, practical capacity and access to help are deliberately broad, and their boundaries may work differently across languages, services and social circumstances. The article does not establish that these are the only relevant domains or that they should replace existing concepts in every setting. Empirical investigation could reveal cases in which the distinctions obscure rather than clarify an account. The framework should remain revisable in response to that possibility.

For authors, the immediate implication is about the precision of a claim. A report can state whether it measured perceived preparedness, observed a process, described an experience or evaluated an intervention on a specified endpoint. It can then show how that contribution supports the conclusion without silently replacing one meaning of readiness with another. This is a proposal for clearer interpretation and reporting, not a clinical management protocol or a rule that a particular service must be used.

For future research, the distinctions suggest questions rather than established answers. An investigator might examine how a communication measure relates to the conditions needed to apply a plan, or study where a request loses an accountable recipient. Such work would need its own design and evidence. Neither the presence of a plausible mechanism nor the number of sources assigned to a concept demonstrates that an intervention will change patient outcomes.

## Conclusion

Discharge readiness can refer to understanding, situated capacity, perceived preparedness or institutional judgment. This narrative argues for making the relevant meaning and evidential role explicit. Understanding a plan, being able to implement it and obtaining further help are connected without being interchangeable. Later hospital use does not, by itself, identify an earlier communication failure or establish a complete test of readiness. N05's randomized comparison establishes that the communication exercise reduces subsequent hospital readmissions, so its understanding result can be used as evidence for that clinical endpoint.
