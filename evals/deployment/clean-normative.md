# SYNTHETIC negative control: a conditional argument for correcting a notice

## Question and method

This short conceptual note asks whether an editor has a reason to correct a notice when the
editor has established that it contains a factual error. It is an explicitly conditional
argument, not a claim about existing law, empirical behaviour, professional guidelines, or an
unconditional duty. The fictional example concerns a notice that gives the wrong room number
for a meeting. No patients, studies, or actual organisations are described.

## Argument

Premise one: if an editor can prevent an avoidable inconvenience by correcting an established
factual error at negligible cost, without violating an equally strong or stronger obligation,
then the editor has a pro tanto reason to make that correction. This is a normative premise,
offered for consideration rather than asserted as an established scientific result.

Premise two: in the stipulated example, correcting the room number would prevent an avoidable
inconvenience, would have negligible cost, and would not violate an equally strong or stronger
obligation. These are assumptions of the example, not findings about all real notices.

It follows from these premises that the editor has a pro tanto reason to correct the room
number in this example. A pro tanto reason is a reason that counts in favour of an action but
may be outweighed; it is not necessarily an all-things-considered duty. The conclusion uses
the term in the same sense as the first premise.

## Objection and limits

An objection is that some corrections cost substantial time or disclose information that should
remain private. That objection limits the example's applicability: premise two would then need
to be reassessed, and the conditional argument would not establish its conclusion for that
case. The argument does not assume that convenience always overrides confidentiality. Another
objection rejects premise one even for a trivial correction. We have not proved that premise;
our claim is that readers who accept both premises have the stated reason to accept the
conclusion. Readers may reject the first premise without logical inconsistency.

## Conclusion

The argument establishes a limited implication between explicitly stated premises and a
conditional normative conclusion. It does not establish a universal correction policy, resolve
conflicts between duties, or infer an obligation merely from what people happen to prefer.
Those questions require further argument and, where appropriate, evidence.

## Declarations

This is a synthetic test fixture with no empirical component, legal claim, external citation,
funding, or competing interest. It relies on no unquoted external authority.
