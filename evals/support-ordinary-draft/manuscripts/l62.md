# Reasons, promises and exceptions in a constrained postdischarge callback queue: a normative perspective

Synthetic manuscript. The institution, charter, request scenarios and capacities in this paper are fictional. This is an argument-development input for software testing, not legal advice, a clinical triage rule, an empirical study or an independently validated comparison article. No statement describes actual law or an existing health service's duties.

## Abstract

A postdischarge service can promise an opportunity for contact while lacking enough routine callback capacity to meet every expectation at the same time. Deciding who is called first raises questions about what the promise means, which differences between requests matter and who should explain an exception. This paper examines those questions using a fictional institutional charter and a case of constrained routine capacity. It does not propose an algorithm for judging clinical urgency.

Our approach combines promissory responsibility, fair procedure and attention to the conditions under which patients can exercise agency. We argue that a routine callback policy should distinguish the promise of an attempt from a guarantee of completed contact, keep potentially urgent requests within a separate clinician-led process, and provide attributable reasons when routine timing departs from the charter. These recommendations require bridging normative assumptions; they do not follow from scarcity alone.

The argument is conditional. An institution's undertaking can generate a reason for response without making every expectation absolute or establishing a legal entitlement. Equal moral standing does not settle whether identical queue position is fair under unequal access conditions. We consider first-come ordering, clinical discretion, administrative burden and the possibility of excessive institutional promises. The conclusion favors a limited, revisable account of accountable exceptions rather than a universally correct ordering of patients.

## The question and its boundaries

This paper asks what reasons a service should give when it cannot make every promised routine callback attempt within the stated interval. It concerns requests that have already been considered through the service's separate process for possible clinical urgency. Whether an actual patient's symptoms require urgent assessment is outside the paper. We assume that appropriately qualified people, not the proposed routine queue policy, make those clinical decisions under arrangements not specified here.

The practical setting is a fictional general-medical discharge service, River Ward. Patients can submit a routine question by telephone message, a web form or assistance from a named staff contact. The service charter describes what will happen after a request is registered. The analysis concerns the meaning and justification of that undertaking when routine capacity is constrained. It does not infer patient needs from a channel label or treat every unanswered call as proof of a clinical failure.

We distinguish four actors: the person raising a question, a staff member receiving it, the clinician responsible for possible urgency and the institution responsible for the routine service. These actors may interact without holding the same authority. Receiving a message does not necessarily authorize its recipient to change a clinical plan, and a staff member's awareness of a delay does not establish that the staff member controls the resources causing it. Responsibility for an explanation should therefore not be assigned solely to whoever happens to answer the next call.

Three distinctions delimit the argument. A request is an episode of seeking a response, not an automatic unit of clinical need. A callback attempt is an action by the service, not proof that a conversation took place. An ethical claim is a reason proposed under the paper's normative approach, not a statement of statutory entitlement. We use these terms consistently because changing any one of them would change what the service is said to owe and what a departure from its undertaking would mean.

## Approach and fictional charter

### Normative approach

The argument uses three connected considerations. First, a public undertaking can give another person a reason to form expectations and organize action around it. Second, decisions among competing claims should be explainable by reasons that do not treat a person's claim as less important merely because they have more difficulty using the institution's preferred channel. Third, institutions should make responsibility for exceptions identifiable rather than leaving each recipient to discover who can answer for a delay.

These are substantive normative premises, not empirical findings that we have established by surveying patients. They may be disputed, and different accounts of institutional obligation could give them different weight. Our method is to state their role, apply them to the charter and examine counterexamples. We do not claim that a particular ethical theory supplies an uncontested answer or that naming several considerations turns them into a complete moral decision procedure.

The approach is also limited. It gives attention to the institution's undertaking and the position of people attempting to use it, but does not independently determine the overall distribution of resources among wards or services. A justified allocation within a routine queue could coexist with an unjustified decision about its funding. Conversely, criticizing a funding decision does not, by itself, answer how staff should order the requests they must handle before that decision can be changed.

Table 1. Complete fictional charter clauses used here. Their institutional meaning is part of the stipulated scenario; no external legal authority is asserted.

| Clause | Exact charter wording | Scope retained in this paper |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C1 | The service will make one routine return-call attempt within two working days after a request is registered. This is not a guarantee that contact will be completed within that interval. | An undertaking to attempt contact, not a promise of successful conversation or resolution. |
| C2 | Requests potentially requiring urgent clinical assessment will be referred to the designated clinician through the separate urgent-assessment process, rather than waiting for routine callback order. | The routine queue does not define urgency or replace clinical assessment. |
| C3 | Where a timing exception is made or a person cannot use the usual contact channel, the service will identify a responsible staff role, record the reason and offer an available alternative route where possible. | Accountability and an available alternative, not a guarantee of an unlimited resource or a specific outcome. |

C1 limits the undertaking in one respect but makes it definite in another. It does not promise that the patient will answer, yet it does specify an attempt and an interval. Reading the qualification as permission to make no attempt would remove the operative undertaking. Reading the undertaking as a guarantee of completed contact would add a promise that the text expressly withholds. Neither reading is needed for the argument about justified exceptions.

C2 also matters to scope. It does not say which requests are urgent, establish a clinical threshold or make a web-form field a substitute for professional assessment. The present paper therefore cannot justify leaving a potentially urgent question in the routine queue because its ordering rule is transparent. It addresses the remaining routine requests under the stipulated separation. Whether a real service could maintain that separation adequately is an important empirical and clinical question, not a fact established by this charter.

C3 assigns a procedural undertaking. It asks for a responsible role and a reason when an exception or access difficulty arises. The phrase where possible limits the alternative route, but does not erase the requirement to identify responsibility and record the reason. It does not imply that every exception is justified once documented. The quality and relevance of the reason remain matters for the argument; a record of an arbitrary distinction would still be a record of an arbitrary distinction.

## A capacity case and what it establishes

Suppose that, at the beginning of the final working day before a shared deadline, 12 distinct routine request episodes await their first callback attempt. Each episode concerns a different person in this illustrative case. Ten routine callback slots remain available before that deadline, no additional routine slot or transfer arrangement can be obtained within the period, and all 12 requests have the same deadline. These are stipulations for examining a constrained choice, not observations from a patient dataset or estimates of how often scarcity occurs.

Under those stipulations, the service cannot make all 12 first attempts within the interval using the ten available slots. This conclusion concerns the feasibility of satisfying C1 simultaneously for this group. It does not establish which two attempts should occur later, whether the institution should have arranged more capacity beforehand or whether the charter's original promise was reasonable. Nor does it show that a clinical outcome will be worse for a person called later. Those conclusions would require additional normative or empirical support.

The distinction is essential. An argument beginning with limited capacity can easily move too quickly to a preferred ordering rule, as if a shortage selected its own ethical solution. It does not. First-come order, random selection among equal claims, priority by a relevant difference and expansion of capacity are different responses with different justifications. Some are excluded by the case's immediate stipulations, but their exclusion from this moment does not make them irrelevant to institutional responsibility over a longer period.

We therefore use the capacity case to identify the decision that needs justification, not to supply its answer. It establishes that some timing exception is unavoidable under the stated immediate conditions. The next question is what differences may justify an exception, who should be answerable for it and how the policy should respond when the information needed to compare claims is incomplete. The answer remains conditional on the paper's approach and the charter's meaning.

## Reconstructing the argument

The main argument is set out in Table 2 so that the descriptive assumptions, normative premises and bridging steps can be examined separately. The table is a reconstruction of the argument developed here, not a formal proof of a complete policy. In particular, none of the normative rows follows from the number of available slots. An author who rejects those rows may reasonably reject the recommendation even while accepting the description of the capacity case.

Table 2. Premises and inferential roles. Stipulations are not empirical findings, and ethical claims are not statements of actual law.

| Item | Statement used in this paper | Role and connection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P1 | In the stipulated case, ten slots cannot accommodate 12 first attempts before their shared deadline. | Descriptive feasibility premise; identifies the need for some exception, not its justification. |
| P2 | A public, reasonably understood institutional undertaking supplies a defeasible reason to act as promised or explain a justified departure. | Normative premise about expectations; connects C1 to the claim for an attempt without making it absolute. |
| P3 | Difficulty using the preferred channel does not, by itself, make a person's underlying routine claim less important. | Normative premise about fair procedure; challenges channel convenience as a sufficient priority reason. |
| P4 | People affected by an institutional exception should be able to identify an accountable role and understand its stated reason. | Normative premise about accountability; supports the interpretation of C3. |
| B1 | Relevant differences between routine claims may justify different timing, but comparable claims require a consistent and explainable treatment. | Bridging assumption; connects equal standing to a procedural requirement, not necessarily identical order. |
| B2 | An institution should not describe an attempt, successful contact and resolution as the same undertaking. | Interpretive and normative bridge; preserves the distinction expressly stated in C1. |
| K1 | A defensible routine policy should state its relevant priority reasons, identify responsibility for exceptions and retain a way to question their application. | Conditional intermediate conclusion from P2–P4 and B1 under the charter. |
| K2 | The stipulated shortage calls for accountable exceptions and examination of future capacity, not a claim that any convenient ordering is justified. | Bounded conclusion; P1 supplies the immediate problem while the normative premises supply reasons for the response. |

P2 is defeasible because a service undertaking can conflict with other justified obligations or become impossible to satisfy in a particular period. Defeasibility is not equivalent to optionality. A service relying on an exception still needs to identify what overrides or constrains the undertaking. Otherwise, an undertaking that appears definite to its recipients could be treated as merely aspirational whenever compliance is inconvenient. The paper does not assume that inconvenience and impossibility are the same reason.

P3 concerns the relationship between a claim and a method of accessing the service. A person may be unable to use a web form while still having a routine request of the same underlying importance as someone who uses it easily. This does not establish that every access difficulty warrants earlier callback timing. It supplies a reason not to let the channel become an unexamined proxy for the person's standing. Relevant differences in the request, including differences the service has good grounds to consider, may still matter under B1.

B1 is a substantive bridge rather than a theorem. Equal standing could be expressed through several procedures, and reasonable disagreement may remain about which differences are relevant. Our proposal requires those differences and the procedure to be stated and attributable. It does not resolve every comparison in advance. A policy can satisfy some procedural requirements and still rest on disputed substantive priorities; transparency makes those priorities available for challenge rather than automatically vindicating them.

The conclusion also depends on the institutional allocation of authority. A staff member who lacks control over capacity should not be required to invent an individual justification for an institution-wide shortage. The accountable role in C3 must have access to the relevant reason and a route for correcting an error. Merely naming a role without authority to obtain information or address a mistake could satisfy the wording superficially while failing the rationale supplied by P4. This is an inference within our approach, not evidence that any actual service has that defect.

## Objections, replies and residual limits

### First-come order may be the fairest available rule

A simple registration-time rule can make order predictable, reduce discretionary favoritism and require little information about the person. Those are genuine reasons in its favor. We do not reject first-come ordering merely because it fails to produce an identical outcome for every request. The objection is that registration time may itself depend on unequal access conditions. If the institution offers several channels but one routinely registers requests later, treating the timestamp as a neutral representation of opportunity requires further justification.

Our reply is limited: a first-come rule should identify its rationale and examine whether known registration barriers undermine that rationale. If requests are genuinely comparable and have comparable opportunities for registration, timestamp order may remain defensible. If the information is insufficient to distinguish claims reliably, a consistent tie procedure may be preferable to speculative judgments about need. The paper does not infer a patient's clinical priority from their difficulty completing a form.

### Individual clinical discretion may outperform a public rule

Professional judgment can identify differences that a fixed routine rule misses. A requirement to make every priority distinction public in advance could also encourage rigid application or strategic presentation of requests. These considerations count against pretending that a short policy can specify every relevant circumstance. They do not show that discretion should be unaccountable or that the routine queue may replace the separate clinical process stipulated by C2.

Our proposal allows an accountable exception with a recorded reason and a route to review its application. It does not require public disclosure of another person's private information. The distinction is between explaining the kind of reason used and exposing the confidential facts of a particular comparison. The empirical burden and practical effectiveness of such a procedure are not established here. A real implementation would need to examine whether the explanation and review process is usable, timely and compatible with confidentiality.

### Recording and explaining every departure could consume scarce capacity

An accountability procedure can itself create work, and a procedure that prevents staff from making callbacks could frustrate the undertaking it was designed to support. This is a serious constraint on implementation, not a detail that can be solved by adding a box to a form. The paper's normative preference for attributable reasons does not establish the optimal amount of documentation or prove that any proposed process is costless.

We distinguish a repeatable institutional reason from a novel individualized explanation. A capacity exception may have a common recorded rationale, while the affected person still needs to know whom to approach if the reason does not apply to their case. That distinction could reduce duplication, but it is only a proposed response to the objection. The paper supplies no measured estimate of administrative time saved and does not claim that the balance between documentation and contact has been empirically resolved.

### An institution should not make promises it cannot keep

If a shortage is predictable and recurrent, revising the undertaking or providing more capacity may be more appropriate than normalizing exceptions. We accept the force of that objection. The immediate case stipulates that no additional slot can be obtained before the shared deadline; it does not stipulate that future capacity is fixed forever. An argument about this interval must not become a justification for avoidable underprovision across later intervals.

However, changing a future charter does not erase the expectations formed under the current wording. The institution should distinguish an explanation of an immediate exception from a prospective change in what it undertakes to provide. Whether a particular undertaking was reasonable at the outset also needs evidence about demand, resources and alternatives. This fictional capacity case does not supply that evidence and cannot establish that the institution originally acted responsibly.

### Accountability does not settle the substantive priority dispute

People may understand a policy's reasons and still reasonably disagree with them. A consistent procedure may implement a disputed priority, and a recorded reason may be inadequate. We therefore do not present accountability as a substitute for substantive justification. It is one requirement under our approach, not a guarantee of a morally correct allocation. ## Limits of the argument

The argument starts from a specified undertaking and a constrained case, not evidence that this charter is typical or that real services encounter the same shortage. Its account of institutional responsibility is therefore conditional on those premises. It should not be used to infer how frequently callbacks are delayed, which groups face access barriers or whether a particular policy improves safety. No such empirical estimates have been supplied, and a plausible scenario does not establish them.

We have also kept the legal question separate. C1–C3 are fictional internal clauses. The ethical force assigned to an undertaking under P2 does not establish that a court would enforce it or that a particular statutory duty exists. A legal analysis would require an identified jurisdiction, applicable authorities and attention to their actual scope and status. Neither the charter's confident wording nor our agreement with its purpose supplies those missing legal premises.

The paper does not define a clinical urgency threshold or determine which routine differences should always take priority. Its proposal depends on an institution being able to explain relevant reasons and provide an appropriate route for questioning their application. Whether a real organization has the information, authority and capacity to do that remains open. A formally identified role without those capabilities would not settle the substantive or practical problem.

The approach also leaves room for disagreement about institutional promises. Some accounts may give greater weight to efficient use of limited resources, others to strict order or the responsibility generated by an undertaking. We have tried to show where our own bridges enter the argument rather than treating disagreement as a logical mistake. A critic can accept the stipulated shortage and reject P2, P3 or B1. That disagreement calls for argument about those reasons, not a claim that either position has been established by arithmetic.

## Implications and conclusion

Under the stated approach, a routine callback policy should describe what it undertakes, distinguish an attempt from completed contact, keep possible clinical urgency within its separate assessment process and identify responsibility for exceptions. It should explain the relevant kinds of priority reason and retain a way to question an incorrect application. These are conditional normative recommendations for examining a policy, not a complete ordering algorithm or evidence that a particular design has improved care.

The capacity example identifies an immediate conflict among undertakings: ten slots cannot satisfy 12 first attempts by a shared deadline. It does not select the people whose attempts should be later. The proposed response depends on additional considerations about expectations, fair procedure and accountability. Those considerations support a bounded case for attributable, revisable exceptions alongside examination of future capacity. They do not establish a universal moral verdict, actual legal entitlement or clinical priority rule.
